🕊️ Right to Freedom — Overview

The Right to Freedom protects important individual freedoms and personal liberty. It is mainly covered under Articles 19 to 22, along with Article 21A.

Articles: 19–22 + 21A
Main Areas: Six freedoms | Criminal protections | Life and personal liberty | Education | Arrest and detention

📜 Article 19 — Six Freedoms

Article 19 guarantees certain basic freedoms to citizens of India. These freedoms are not absolute and are subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by law.

Article 19(1)(a) — Freedom of Speech and Expression
Citizens have the right to express their views and opinions through speech, writing, printing, visual representation and other forms of communication.
Article 19(1)(b) — Right to Assemble Peacefully
Citizens have the right to assemble peacefully and without arms. The State may impose reasonable restrictions in the interests of public order, sovereignty and integrity of India.
Article 19(1)(c) — Right to Form Associations or Unions
Citizens have the right to form associations, unions and co-operative societies.
Article 19(1)(d) — Freedom of Movement
Citizens have the right to move freely throughout the territory of India.
Article 19(1)(e) — Right to Residence
Citizens have the right to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India.
Article 19(1)(g) — Profession, Occupation, Trade or Business
Citizens have the right to practise any profession or to carry on any occupation, trade or business, subject to reasonable restrictions and professional requirements prescribed by law.
Important: Article 19 originally contained seven freedoms. The Right to Property under Article 19(1)(f) was removed by the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978. Therefore, Article 19 now contains six freedoms.

⚖️ Reasonable Restrictions under Article 19

The freedoms under Article 19 are subject to restrictions prescribed by the Constitution. The purpose is to balance individual liberty with public interest and national security.

Article 19(2):
Restrictions on freedom of speech and expression may be imposed in the interests of:
  • Sovereignty and integrity of India
  • Security of the State
  • Friendly relations with foreign States
  • Public order
  • Decency or morality
  • Contempt of court
  • Defamation
  • Incitement to an offence
Article 19(3):
Reasonable restrictions may be imposed on peaceful assembly in the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India or public order.
Article 19(4):
Reasonable restrictions may be imposed on associations or unions in the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, public order, morality.
Article 19(5):
Reasonable restrictions may be imposed on movement and residence in the interests of the general public or for the protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.
Article 19(6):
Reasonable restrictions may be imposed on profession, occupation, trade or business in the interests of the general public. The State may also prescribe professional or technical qualifications and may carry on a trade, business, industry or service either itself or through a corporation.

🛡️ Article 20 — Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences

Article 20 provides important safeguards to persons accused or convicted of offences. It contains three constitutional protections.

Article 20(1) — Protection Against Ex-post-facto Criminal Law

A person cannot be convicted for an act which was not an offence when it was committed.

Similarly, a person cannot be subjected to a greater penalty than the penalty prescribed by law at the time the offence was committed.

Simple Example:
Suppose an act was legal in 2024 but a law makes that act a criminal offence in 2025. A person cannot be punished under the 2025 law for doing that act in 2024.
Article 20(2) — Protection Against Double Jeopardy

No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.

This means that after a person has been prosecuted and punished for an offence, the Constitution protects that person from being punished again for the same offence.

Exam Point:
Article 20(2) specifically uses the principle of double jeopardy.
Article 20(3) — Protection Against Self-Incrimination

No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

In simple terms, an accused person cannot be forced to provide testimonial evidence that would directly incriminate himself.

Remember:
Article 20(3) applies to a person accused of an offence and protects against compelled self-incriminating testimony.
Three protections under Article 20:

20(1) → No ex-post-facto criminal law
20(2) → No double jeopardy
20(3) → No self-incrimination

❤️ Article 21 — Protection of Life and Personal Liberty

Article 21 states that:

No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

The expression "life" has been interpreted broadly by the Supreme Court. It does not merely mean physical existence; it has been connected with the right to live with dignity and other essential aspects of a meaningful life.

Important

Personal Liberty

The protection extends beyond mere physical freedom and has been given a broad interpretation by the Supreme Court.

Judicial Development

Fair Procedure

Through judicial interpretation, the procedure affecting life or personal liberty must satisfy constitutional standards of fairness and reasonableness.

🎓 Article 21A — Right to Education

Article 21A provides the Fundamental Right to Education for children between 6 and 14 years.

The State shall provide free and compulsory education to all children of the age of six to fourteen years in such manner as the State may determine by law.

Article 21A was inserted by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 provides the legislative framework for this right.

🚔 Article 22 — Protection Against Arrest and Detention

Article 22 provides safeguards to persons who are arrested or detained. It also contains special provisions relating to preventive detention.

Article 22(1)

Information and Legal Representation

A person who is arrested shall be informed, as soon as possible, of the grounds of arrest and shall not be denied the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of his choice.

Article 22(2)

Production Before Magistrate

Every arrested person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours, excluding the time necessary for travelling from the place of arrest to the court.

No person can be detained beyond this period without the authority of a magistrate.

Article 22(3):
The protections under clauses (1) and (2) do not apply to:

(a) an enemy alien; or

(b) a person arrested or detained under a law providing for preventive detention.
Article 22(4):
Preventive detention beyond the constitutionally prescribed period requires the safeguards specified by the Constitution, including the role of an Advisory Board as provided by the clause.
Article 22(5):
A person detained under preventive detention must be communicated the grounds of detention as soon as possible and given the earliest opportunity of making a representation against the detention.
Article 22(6):
The detaining authority may withhold facts which it considers against the public interest to disclose.
Article 22(7):
Parliament may by law prescribe matters relating to preventive detention, including the circumstances and classes of cases in which a person may be detained beyond the period specified by the Constitution, subject to the constitutional requirements.
Preventive vs Punitive Detention:
Punitive detention is generally detention after a person is found to have committed an offence. Preventive detention is detention intended to prevent a person from doing something considered harmful to specified interests in the future.

⚖️ Important Supreme Court Judgments

1. A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950)

Article: 21

Judgment: The Supreme Court initially adopted a narrower interpretation of Article 21 and treated "procedure established by law" distinctly from the broader American concept of due process.

Exam Point: An important early interpretation of Article 21.

2. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

Articles: 14, 19 and 21

Judgment: The Supreme Court significantly expanded the protection of personal liberty and held that the procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty must satisfy constitutional requirements of fairness and reasonableness.

Exam Point: Articles 14, 19 and 21 were read together as providing a strong constitutional protection of personal liberty.

3. Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015)

Article: 19(1)(a)

Judgment: The Supreme Court struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 for violating the freedom of speech and expression guaranteed under Article 19(1)(a).

Exam Point: A major judgment concerning freedom of speech in the digital age.

4. Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)

Article: 21

Judgment: A nine-judge Constitution Bench recognised the right to privacy as a Fundamental Right protected by the Constitution, particularly within the guarantees of Articles 14, 19 and 21.

Exam Point: Right to Privacy → Fundamental Right.

5. A.K. Roy v. Union of India (1982)

Article: 22

Judgment: The Supreme Court examined constitutional safeguards and procedural requirements relating to preventive detention.

Exam Point: Important case for understanding preventive detention under Article 22.

6. Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993)

Article: 21

Judgment: The Supreme Court recognised the right to education as flowing from the right to life and personal liberty, subject to the scope identified by the Court. This judicial development later contributed to the constitutional insertion of Article 21A.

Exam Point: Important judicial development behind the constitutional recognition of the Right to Education.

📚 Important Constitutional Amendments

44th Amendment Act, 1978
Right to Property was removed from the list of Fundamental Rights. Article 19(1)(f) was omitted.
86th Amendment Act, 2002
Inserted Article 21A — Right to Education for children aged 6–14 years.
