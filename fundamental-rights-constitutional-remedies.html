🛡️ Right to Constitutional Remedies — Overview

Fundamental Rights are meaningful only when there is a way to protect them. The Constitution therefore provides a special remedy through Article 32.

Article 32 gives a person the right to approach the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights when they are violated.

Article: 32
Main Purpose: Protection and enforcement of Fundamental Rights
Constitutional Remedy: Writs

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the "heart and soul of the Constitution" because it provides a direct constitutional remedy for the protection of Fundamental Rights.

⚖️ Article 32 — Constitutional Remedies

Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right. It allows a person to directly approach the Supreme Court when a Fundamental Right has been violated.

Article 32(1)

The right to move the Supreme Court by appropriate proceedings for the enforcement of the rights conferred by Part III is guaranteed.

Simple meaning: If your Fundamental Right is violated, you have a constitutional right to approach the Supreme Court for its protection.

Article 32(2)

The Supreme Court has the power to issue directions, orders or writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

The Constitution specifically mentions five writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto.

Article 32(3)

Parliament may by law empower any other court to exercise, within its local jurisdiction, all or any of the powers exercisable by the Supreme Court under Article 32(2).

Simple meaning: Parliament can give such writ powers to other courts as provided by law.

Article 32(4)

The right guaranteed by Article 32 shall not be suspended except as otherwise provided by the Constitution.

This means the right under Article 32 has strong constitutional protection, although the Constitution itself provides limited situations in which enforcement of certain Fundamental Rights may be affected.

📜 The Five Constitutional Writs

A writ is a formal order issued by a constitutional court to protect rights or control the exercise of legal powers.

The easiest way to understand the five writs is to remember what each one tries to do.

Writ 1

🔵 Habeas Corpus

Meaning: "Produce the person."

Habeas Corpus is mainly used to protect a person from unlawful detention.

Easy Example:
Suppose the police detain Rahul without lawful authority. Rahul's family can approach the court. The court may require the detaining authority to produce Rahul before it and examine whether the detention is lawful.

If the detention is found to be unlawful, the court can order the person's release.

Remember:
Habeas Corpus → Protection against unlawful detention
Writ 2

🟢 Mandamus

Meaning: "We command."

Mandamus is issued by a court to command a public authority, public official or other body performing a public duty to perform a duty required by law.

Easy Example:
Suppose a public authority is legally required to consider an application but deliberately refuses to perform its duty. The affected person may approach the court, and the court may direct the authority to perform its legal duty.

Mandamus is therefore mainly connected with the failure to perform a public/legal duty.

Remember:
Mandamus → Perform a legal/public duty
Writ 3

🟡 Prohibition

Meaning: "To forbid" or "Stop."

Prohibition is issued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal when it is proceeding in a matter beyond its jurisdiction or contrary to law.

Easy Example:
Suppose a tribunal is hearing a matter which legally does not fall within its jurisdiction. Before the tribunal completes the case, the higher court may order it to stop the proceedings.
Remember:
Prohibition → Stop a lower court/tribunal from continuing an unlawful proceeding
Writ 4

🟠 Certiorari

Meaning: "To be certified" / "To send the record."

Certiorari is used by a higher court to review the decision or proceedings of a lower court or tribunal and, where legally appropriate, quash its order.

Easy Example:
Suppose a tribunal decides a case even though it had no jurisdiction to decide it. The affected person may approach the higher court. If the legal requirements are satisfied, the higher court can quash the tribunal's order.
Remember:
Certiorari → Review and quash an unlawful order
Writ 5

🔴 Quo Warranto

Meaning: "By what authority?"

Quo Warranto is used to question the legal authority of a person who occupies a public office.

Easy Example:
Suppose a person is appointed to a public office but does not satisfy the legal qualifications required for that office. A court can be asked to examine the authority under which that person is holding the office.

If the appointment is legally invalid, the court can prevent the person from continuing to hold the office.

Remember:
Quo Warranto → Question the authority to hold a public office

🧠 Prohibition vs Certiorari

Prohibition

🛑 Stop

Used to prevent a lower court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction.

Think: "Don't continue."

Certiorari

❌ Quash

Used to review and, where appropriate, quash an order of a lower court or tribunal.

Think: "The order is being removed."

🏛️ Article 32 vs Article 226

Article 32

Supreme Court

The Supreme Court can issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right.

Article 226

High Courts

High Courts can issue writs for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights as well as for other legal rights.

Therefore, the writ jurisdiction of High Courts under Article 226 is wider in scope.

Easy Memory:
Article 32 → Supreme Court → Fundamental Rights
Article 226 → High Court → Fundamental Rights + Other Legal Rights

⚠️ Can Article 32 Be Suspended?

Article 32 has strong constitutional protection, but the Constitution itself provides provisions concerning the enforcement of Fundamental Rights during an Emergency.

Article 359:

During a Proclamation of Emergency, the President may by order suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of such Fundamental Rights as may be specified in the order, subject to the constitutional conditions.

Important:
The Constitution specifically protects the enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 from suspension under Article 359.

⚖️ Important Supreme Court Judgments

1. Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950)

What happened?
The government restricted the circulation of a journal, and the case involved the protection of freedom of speech under Article 19.

Supreme Court's decision:
The Supreme Court emphasised the importance of Fundamental Rights and held that where a Fundamental Right is violated, Article 32 provides a direct remedy before the Supreme Court.

Exam Point:
Article 32 provides a direct constitutional remedy for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

2. Daryao v. State of U.P. (1961)

What happened?
The case concerned the relationship between proceedings before High Courts under Article 226 and proceedings before the Supreme Court under Article 32.

Supreme Court's decision:
The Court recognised the principle of res judicata in constitutional writ proceedings, meaning that a matter already finally decided by a competent court cannot ordinarily be re-agitated between the same parties.

Exam Point:
Important case concerning the relationship between Articles 32 and 226.

3. L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

What happened?
The case concerned the power of judicial review and the role of High Courts and the Supreme Court in reviewing decisions of tribunals.

Supreme Court's decision:
The Supreme Court held that judicial review by the High Courts under Article 226/227 and by the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the Basic Structure of the Constitution.

Exam Point:
Judicial review under Articles 32 and 226 → Basic Structure.

4. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)

What happened?
Maneka Gandhi's passport was impounded by the government, raising important questions about personal liberty and the relationship between Fundamental Rights.

Supreme Court's decision:
The Court gave a broad interpretation to Article 21 and held that the procedure affecting personal liberty must be fair, just and reasonable. It also emphasised the close relationship between Articles 14, 19 and 21.

Exam Point:
Major expansion of the meaning of personal liberty and constitutional protection.

5. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

What happened?
The case challenged constitutional amendments that attempted to give very wide protection to certain Directive Principles against challenge based on Fundamental Rights.

Supreme Court's decision:
The Supreme Court held that limited government, judicial review and the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are important parts of the constitutional structure.

Exam Point:
Judicial review is an important part of the Basic Structure.

🎯 Quick Revision

Article 32: Right to approach the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
Article 32(1): Right to move the Supreme Court.
Article 32(2): Supreme Court can issue directions, orders and writs.
Article 32(3): Parliament may empower other courts with specified powers.
Article 32(4): Right under Article 32 cannot be suspended except as provided by the Constitution.
Five Writs:

Habeas Corpus → Unlawful detention
Mandamus → Perform public/legal duty
Prohibition → Stop lower court/tribunal
Certiorari → Review and quash order
Quo Warranto → Question authority to hold public office
Golden Line:
Article 32 protects Fundamental Rights through constitutional remedies, while Article 226 gives High Courts a wider writ jurisdiction.
