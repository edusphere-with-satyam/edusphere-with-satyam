Unit 1 — Introduction to Microeconomics

This unit introduces the basic concepts of economics and explains how individuals, firms and economies make choices when resources are limited and have alternative uses.

Key Idea:

Human wants are unlimited, but resources are scarce and have alternative uses. Therefore, every individual and economy has to make choices.

1. What is Economics?

Definition 1: Economics is the study of how people and society make choices about the use of scarce resources that have alternative uses.

Definition 2: Economics is a social science that studies how individuals, firms and governments use limited resources to satisfy human wants.

In simple words: Human wants are unlimited, but resources are limited. Economics studies how we make choices about using these limited resources.

Example: A student has limited time before an examination. The student may have to choose between studying Economics, Mathematics or another subject. Choosing one activity means giving up another activity.

2. Microeconomics

Definition: Microeconomics is the branch of economics that studies the behaviour and decisions of individual consumers, firms and particular markets.

Microeconomics mainly deals with individual economic units and their economic decisions.

Examples:

  • Demand for a particular product
  • Price of a particular commodity
  • Production decision of a firm
  • Consumer's choice between two goods
  • Price determination in a particular market

3. Macroeconomics

Definition: Macroeconomics is the branch of economics that studies the economy as a whole.

It deals with aggregate economic variables such as national income, employment, general price level and economic growth.

Examples:

  • National income
  • Unemployment
  • Inflation
  • Economic growth
  • Overall level of employment

4. Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

Microeconomics Macroeconomics
Studies individual economic units Studies the economy as a whole
Studies individual consumers Studies aggregate consumption
Studies individual firms Studies aggregate production
Studies price of a particular good Studies general price level
Studies individual markets Studies the overall economy

Easy way to remember: Micro = Individual or small units | Macro = Economy as a whole

5. Positive and Normative Economics

Positive Economics: Positive economics deals with statements that describe facts and cause-and-effect relationships that can be examined using evidence.

Example: An increase in the price of a product may reduce its quantity demanded, other things remaining constant.

Normative Economics: Normative economics deals with value judgements about what ought to be or what should happen.

Example: The government should provide free education to all students.

Remember:

Positive Economics → What is / What happens

Normative Economics → What ought to be / What should happen

6. What is an Economy?

An economy is a system in which people, households, firms and government engage in activities such as production, consumption, exchange and distribution of goods and services.

An economy uses available resources to satisfy human wants.

7. Basic Economic Problem

The basic economic problem arises because human wants are unlimited, while resources available to satisfy those wants are scarce. Moreover, resources have alternative uses.

Basic Economic Problem:

Unlimited Wants + Scarce Resources + Alternative Uses = Economic Problem

Because resources are scarce, individuals, firms and governments have to make choices about how resources should be used.

8. Scarcity

Definition: Scarcity means that resources are limited in relation to unlimited human wants.

Examples:

  • A student's time is limited.
  • A family's income is limited.
  • Land is limited.
  • Natural resources are limited.
  • Capital available to a business is limited.

Scarcity vs Shortage: Scarcity is a fundamental economic problem because resources are limited relative to wants. Shortage generally refers to a situation where the quantity available is insufficient to meet demand under particular market conditions.

9. Choice

Because resources are scarce and have alternative uses, individuals and economies must make choices.

Choice means selecting one option from the available alternatives.

Example: A student has ₹500 and wants both a book and headphones. If the student cannot afford both, a choice has to be made.

Choice is closely related to scarcity and opportunity cost.

10. Opportunity Cost

Definition: Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative foregone when a choice is made.

In simple words, opportunity cost is what we give up when we choose one alternative over another.

Example: Suppose a student has two hours and chooses to study Economics instead of watching a movie. If watching the movie was the next best alternative, the movie experience given up is the opportunity cost of studying Economics.

Important:

Opportunity cost is not necessarily money. It may be time, leisure, income, another product or another activity.

11. Production Possibility Frontier (PPF)

Definition: A Production Possibility Frontier (PPF) is a curve showing the maximum possible combinations of two goods that an economy can produce with given resources and technology, assuming resources are fully and efficiently used.

For example, an economy may produce consumer goods and capital goods. If more resources are used to produce consumer goods, fewer resources may be available for producing capital goods.

12. Assumptions of PPF

  • The economy produces two goods.
  • Resources are given.
  • Technology remains constant.
  • Resources are fully employed.
  • Resources are used efficiently.
  • Resources may not be equally suitable for producing both goods.

13. Points on, Inside and Outside the PPF

Point on the PPF: Represents a combination that can be produced using resources fully and efficiently.

Point inside the PPF: Represents an inefficient or underutilised combination because available resources are not being fully or efficiently used.

Point outside the PPF: Represents a combination that is currently unattainable with the given resources and technology.

On PPF → Efficient

Inside PPF → Inefficient / Underutilisation

Outside PPF → Unattainable

14. Opportunity Cost and PPF

When an economy moves along the PPF and produces more of one good, it generally has to reduce production of the other good. The quantity of the second good sacrificed represents the opportunity cost.

Combination Consumer Goods Capital Goods
A 0 10
B 1 9
C 2 7
D 3 4
E 4 0

Moving from A to B gives up 1 unit of capital goods to obtain 1 additional unit of consumer goods.

Moving from C to D gives up 3 units of capital goods to obtain 1 additional unit of consumer goods.

15. Central Problems of an Economy

Every economy faces three central problems because resources are scarce and have alternative uses.

What to Produce?

An economy has to decide which goods and services should be produced and in what quantities.

For example, an economy may have to decide how much food, clothing, education, healthcare or infrastructure should be produced.

How to Produce?

An economy has to decide which technique of production should be used.

  • Labour-intensive technique
  • Capital-intensive technique

The choice may depend on the availability and cost of labour, capital and technology.

For Whom to Produce?

An economy has to determine how the produced goods and services will be distributed among different people.

This problem is closely related to income and purchasing power.

Remember:

What → Selection of goods and quantities

How → Technique of production

For Whom → Distribution of output

16. Economic Activities

Economic activities are activities related to the production, consumption, exchange and distribution of goods and services.

Production: Creating goods and services or adding utility to resources.

Consumption: Using goods and services to satisfy human wants.

Exchange: Transfer of goods and services between parties, generally through buying and selling.

Distribution: The process through which income generated from production is distributed among factors of production.

17. Economic Systems

An economic system is the arrangement through which an economy organises economic activities and allocates its resources.

Market Economy: Economic decisions are largely influenced by market forces such as demand and supply.

Centrally Planned Economy: Major economic decisions are taken by a central authority or government.

Mixed Economy: A mixed economy combines market mechanisms with government involvement.

India is generally described as a mixed economy.

18. Real-Life Applications

Example 1 — Student's Time: A student has limited time and must choose between studying, playing and entertainment. The alternative given up represents opportunity cost.

Example 2 — Government Budget: The government has limited resources and must decide how much to allocate to education, healthcare, infrastructure and other needs.

Example 3 — Business: A firm with limited capital may have to choose between buying new machinery and expanding its existing premises.

19. Important Terms — Quick Revision

  • Economics: Study of choices involving scarce resources.
  • Microeconomics: Study of individual economic units.
  • Macroeconomics: Study of the economy as a whole.
  • Scarcity: Limited resources in relation to unlimited wants.
  • Choice: Selection among available alternatives.
  • Opportunity Cost: Value of the next best alternative foregone.
  • PPF: Maximum possible combinations of two goods with given resources and technology.
  • Positive Economics: Deals with facts and testable economic relationships.
  • Normative Economics: Deals with value judgements about what ought to be.
  • Economic Problem: Problem arising because resources are scarce and have alternative uses.
