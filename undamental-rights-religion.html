🕊️ Right to Freedom of Religion — Overview

The Right to Freedom of Religion is guaranteed under Articles 25 to 28 of the Constitution. It protects freedom of conscience and permits individuals and religious denominations to follow and manage religious affairs, subject to constitutional limitations.

Articles: 25–28
Main Areas: Freedom of conscience | Religious practice | Religious institutions | Religious denominations | Secular activities | Religious instruction

These rights are an important part of India's constitutional commitment to religious freedom and secularism. However, religious freedom is not absolute and is subject to the limitations provided by the Constitution.

🙏 Article 25 — Freedom of Conscience and Religion

Article 25 gives all persons freedom of conscience and the right to freely profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to constitutional restrictions.

Article 25(1):

All persons are equally entitled to freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion.

This freedom is subject to:

  • Public order
  • Morality
  • Health
  • Other provisions of Part III of the Constitution
Profess

Freedom to Profess

The freedom to openly declare and express one's religious belief and faith.

Practise

Freedom to Practise

The freedom to perform religious practices, rituals and observances, subject to constitutional restrictions.

Propagate

Freedom to Propagate

The freedom to spread or communicate one's religious beliefs to others.

Conscience

Freedom of Conscience

The inner freedom of a person to hold, change or follow religious beliefs according to conscience.

Article 25(2)(a):

The State may make laws regulating or restricting any economic, financial, political or other secular activity which may be associated with religious practice.

This means that activities having a secular or non-religious character can be regulated by the State even when they are connected with a religious institution or practice.

Article 25(2)(b):

The State may make laws providing for social welfare and reform or for throwing open Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections of Hindus.

Explanation I:
The wearing and carrying of kirpans is deemed to be included in the profession of the Sikh religion.
Explanation II:
For Article 25(2)(b), the reference to Hindus includes persons professing the Sikh, Jaina and Buddhist religions, and references to Hindu religious institutions are construed accordingly.

🏛️ Article 26 — Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

Article 26 protects the collective rights of a religious denomination or any section of it.

Unlike Article 25, which mainly protects the individual, Article 26 primarily protects the rights of religious denominations.

Article 26 is subject to:
Public order | Morality | Health
Article 26(a):

Every religious denomination or section thereof has the right to establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes.

Article 26(b):

Every religious denomination has the right to manage its own affairs in matters of religion.

This protects the autonomy of a religious denomination in matters that are genuinely religious in character.

Article 26(c):

Every religious denomination has the right to own and acquire movable and immovable property.

Article 26(d):

Every religious denomination has the right to administer such property in accordance with law.

Important distinction:
Article 25 → Individual religious freedom
Article 26 → Collective rights of religious denominations

💰 Article 27 — Freedom from Religious Taxation

Article 27 protects persons from being compelled to pay a tax whose proceeds are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of a particular religion or religious denomination.

Article 27:

No person shall be compelled to pay any taxes, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated for the payment of expenses for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.

Important Concept: Article 27 deals with a tax specifically imposed and appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of a particular religion. It does not mean that the State can never spend public money on activities connected with religious institutions or places. The constitutional issue depends upon the nature and purpose of the levy and expenditure.

🏫 Article 28 — Religious Instruction in Educational Institutions

Article 28 deals with religious instruction and religious worship in educational institutions.

Article 28(1):

No religious instruction shall be provided in any educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds.

Article 28(2):

Article 28(1) does not apply to an educational institution which is administered by the State but has been established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction to be imparted in that institution.

Article 28(3):

No person attending an educational institution recognised by the State or receiving aid out of State funds shall be required to take part in religious instruction or religious worship conducted in the institution or attached premises unless the person, or in the case of a minor, the guardian, has given consent.

Article 28(4):
There is no clause (4) in Article 28. Article 28 contains only clauses (1), (2) and (3). This is important because we are keeping the constitutional clause numbering exact.

🔍 Article 25 vs Article 26

Article 25

Individual Right

Protects freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion.

Article 26

Collective Right

Protects the rights of religious denominations to establish institutions, manage religious affairs and administer property.

📚 Important Constitutional Concepts

Religion:
The Constitution protects religious belief and practice, but religious freedom operates within the constitutional framework.
Secular Activity:
The State can regulate economic, financial, political and other secular activities associated with religious practice under Article 25(2)(a).
Religious Denomination:
Article 26 protects a religious denomination or a section of it as a collective body in matters covered by the provision.
Essential Religious Practices:
The Supreme Court has developed the doctrine of essential religious practices while examining whether a claimed practice receives constitutional protection.

⚖️ Important Supreme Court Judgments

1. The Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Sri Shirur Mutt (1954)

Articles: 25 and 26

Judgment: The Supreme Court explained the scope of religious freedom and recognised that matters of religion can receive constitutional protection. It also distinguished religious matters from secular administration associated with religious institutions.

Exam Point: Known as the Shirur Mutt case and important for the development of the Essential Religious Practices doctrine.

2. Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977)

Article: 25

Judgment: The Supreme Court held that the word "propagate" in Article 25 means the right to spread or communicate one's religion, but it does not include a fundamental right to convert another person.

Exam Point: Right to propagate ≠ Fundamental Right to convert another person.

3. Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986)

Article: 25

Judgment: The Supreme Court protected school students who, because of their conscientious religious belief, respectfully declined to sing the National Anthem. The Court found that they had not shown disrespect and protected their freedom of conscience.

Exam Point: Freedom of conscience can protect a person's decision not to participate in an act contrary to sincerely held religious belief, provided constitutional requirements are respected.

4. S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

Constitutional Principle: Secularism

Judgment: The Supreme Court recognised secularism as a basic feature of the Constitution. The State must maintain constitutional equality and cannot identify itself with or favour a particular religion.

Exam Point: Secularism → Basic Structure of the Constitution.

5. Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (2018)

Article: 25

Judgment: In the Sabarimala case, the Constitution Bench held that the exclusion of women of the specified age group from the temple violated constitutional guarantees, including equality and religious freedom principles. The judgment examined the conflict between claimed religious practices and constitutional rights.

Exam Point: Important case concerning the relationship between religious practices and constitutional equality.

6. Ismail Faruqui v. Union of India (1994)

Articles: 25 and 26

Judgment: The Supreme Court examined the relationship between religious freedom and property/state acquisition in the context of places of worship. It held that while religious practice is protected, the right to property in a particular place of worship is not itself an absolute Fundamental Right under Articles 25 and 26.

Exam Point: Religious freedom does not automatically create an absolute Fundamental Right to a particular property.
