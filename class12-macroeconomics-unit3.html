📚 Section 1 – Basic Concepts, Aggregate Demand & Aggregate Supply

1. Meaning of Income and Employment

National Income: It is the money value of all final goods and services produced by the normal residents of a country during a financial year.

In macroeconomics, the level of income and employment is determined by the interaction between Aggregate Demand (AD) and Aggregate Supply (AS).

Keynes explained that the level of employment in an economy depends mainly on the level of effective demand.

2. Aggregate Demand (AD)

Aggregate Demand refers to the total demand for goods and services in an economy during a given period.

In a simple two-sector economy:

AD = C + I

Where:

  • C = Consumption expenditure
  • I = Investment expenditure

In a three-sector economy:

AD = C + I + G

In an open economy:

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
  • G = Government expenditure
  • X = Exports
  • M = Imports

3. Components of Aggregate Demand

Component Meaning
Consumption (C) Expenditure by households on goods and services.
Investment (I) Expenditure on capital goods, inventories and productive assets.
Government Expenditure (G) Government spending on public goods, services and development activities.
Net Exports (X − M) Difference between exports and imports.

4. Aggregate Supply (AS)

Aggregate Supply refers to the total quantity of goods and services produced in an economy during a given period.

In the Keynesian short-run model, when prices are assumed to be given, the value of aggregate supply is represented by the level of income.

AS = Y

Here Y represents national income/output.

5. Effective Demand

Effective Demand is the level of aggregate demand at which AD = AS.

According to Keynes, the equilibrium level of employment is determined by effective demand.

Effective Demand → AD = AS
📌 Board Point:

An increase in aggregate demand can increase the level of output and employment when the economy is operating below full employment.

6. Ex-Ante and Ex-Post Concepts

Ex-Ante Ex-Post
Refers to planned or intended values. Refers to actual or realised values.
Determined before the activity takes place. Determined after the activity takes place.
Example: Planned investment. Example: Actual investment.

7. Equilibrium Level of Income

An economy is in equilibrium when the planned aggregate demand is equal to the level of aggregate supply/output.

AD = AS

Since AS = Y:

Y = AD

For a two-sector economy:

Y = C + I
⭐ Quick Revision – Section 1
  • AD represents total planned expenditure.
  • AS represents total output/income.
  • In a two-sector economy, AD = C + I.
  • Equilibrium occurs when AD = AS.
  • Effective demand determines equilibrium employment.
  • In the simple Keynesian model, AS = Y.

💰 Section 2 – Consumption, Saving & Investment

1. Consumption Function

The Consumption Function shows the functional relationship between consumption expenditure and income.

Consumption generally increases when income increases, but the increase in consumption is usually less than the increase in income.

C = C̄ + bY
  • C = Consumption expenditure
  • C̄ = Autonomous consumption
  • b = Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)
  • Y = Income

2. Autonomous and Induced Consumption

Autonomous Consumption Induced Consumption
Does not depend on current income. Depends on the level of income.
Exists even when income is zero. Changes with changes in income.
Example: Basic consumption financed through past savings. Example: Higher spending due to higher income.

3. Saving Function

Saving is the part of income that is not spent on consumption.
S = Y − C

Using the consumption function:

S = −C̄ + (1 − b)Y

4. Average Propensity to Consume (APC)

APC measures the proportion of income spent on consumption.
APC = C / Y

Where C is consumption and Y is income.

5. Average Propensity to Save (APS)

APS measures the proportion of income saved.
APS = S / Y

6. Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)

MPC measures the change in consumption resulting from a change in income.
MPC = ΔC / ΔY

If income increases by ₹100 and consumption increases by ₹80:

Example:
ΔY = ₹100
ΔC = ₹80

MPC = 80 / 100 = 0.8

7. Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)

MPS measures the change in saving resulting from a change in income.
MPS = ΔS / ΔY

Important relationship:

MPC + MPS = 1

8. Relationship between APC and APS

APC + APS = 1

Because income is either consumed or saved:

Y = C + S

9. Important Relationships

Concept Formula
Average Propensity to Consume APC = C/Y
Average Propensity to Save APS = S/Y
Marginal Propensity to Consume MPC = ΔC/ΔY
Marginal Propensity to Save MPS = ΔS/ΔY
Relationship MPC + MPS = 1
Relationship APC + APS = 1

10. Investment

Investment means expenditure on capital goods and inventories which helps to increase productive capacity.

Investment may be classified as:

  • Autonomous investment
  • Induced investment
Autonomous Investment: Investment which is independent of the level of income.
Induced Investment: Investment which changes with changes in income or demand.
📌 Board Points – Section 2
  • Consumption and saving are positively related to income.
  • MPC lies between 0 and 1 in the standard Keynesian framework.
  • MPC + MPS = 1.
  • APC + APS = 1.
  • APC may be greater than 1 when consumption exceeds income.
  • APS can be negative when saving is negative.
  • Autonomous consumption can exist even at zero income.
  • Investment is an important component of aggregate demand.

📈 Section 3 – Income Determination & Investment Multiplier

1. Two-Sector Model

A two-sector economy consists of:

  • Households
  • Firms

There is no government sector and no foreign sector in this simple model.

AD = C + I

2. Equilibrium through AD and AS

Equilibrium income is determined at the point where aggregate demand is equal to aggregate supply.

AD = AS

Since:

AS = Y

Therefore:

Y = C + I

3. Saving-Investment Approach

In a two-sector economy, equilibrium can also be determined through equality between planned saving and planned investment.

S = I

Therefore, two important conditions of equilibrium are:

AD = AS
S = I

4. Numerical Example of Equilibrium Income

Suppose:

C = 100 + 0.8Y
I = 200

At equilibrium:
Y = C + I

Therefore:
Y = 100 + 0.8Y + 200
Y = 300 + 0.8Y
Y − 0.8Y = 300
0.2Y = 300

Y = ₹1500

5. Investment Multiplier

The Investment Multiplier shows how many times the level of income increases as a result of an initial increase in investment.
K = ΔY / ΔI

In terms of MPC:

K = 1 / (1 − MPC)

Since:

MPS = 1 − MPC

Therefore:

K = 1 / MPS

6. Working of the Multiplier

When investment increases, income increases. The increase in income leads to an increase in consumption. The increase in consumption creates further income for others, which again leads to further consumption.

This process continues in successive rounds until the total increase in income becomes a multiple of the initial increase in investment.

Example:

If MPC = 0.8:
K = 1 / (1 − 0.8)
K = 1 / 0.2
K = 5

If investment increases by ₹100 crore:
ΔY = K × ΔI
ΔY = 5 × ₹100 crore
ΔY = ₹500 crore

7. Relationship between MPC and Multiplier

MPC MPS Multiplier
0.5 0.5 2
0.6 0.4 2.5
0.75 0.25 4
0.8 0.2 5
0.9 0.1 10
Important: As MPC increases, MPS decreases and the value of the multiplier increases.

8. Multiplier Process – Step by Step

  1. There is an initial increase in investment.
  2. The increase in investment increases income.
  3. People spend a part of their additional income.
  4. This additional consumption becomes income for other people.
  5. They again spend a part of their additional income.
  6. The process continues through successive rounds.
  7. The total increase in income becomes a multiple of the initial increase in investment.

9. Important Multiplier Formulae

K = ΔY / ΔI
K = 1 / (1 − MPC)
K = 1 / MPS
ΔY = K × ΔI

10. Paradox of Thrift

The Paradox of Thrift refers to a situation where an attempt by all people to increase saving may lead to a decrease in aggregate demand and income, causing total saving in the economy to remain unchanged or even fall.

If households reduce consumption in order to save more, aggregate demand may fall. Lower demand can reduce production and income.

📌 Board Point: Saving is beneficial for an individual, but if everyone simultaneously tries to save more during a recession, it may reduce aggregate demand and national income.
⭐ Section 3 – Board Revision
  • Equilibrium condition: AD = AS.
  • In a two-sector model: Y = C + I.
  • Alternative equilibrium condition: S = I.
  • Multiplier: K = 1/MPS.
  • Multiplier also equals 1/(1 − MPC).
  • Higher MPC means a higher multiplier.
  • Investment creates a multiplied increase in income.
  • Paradox of thrift is related to excessive saving and reduction in aggregate demand.

🏛️ Section 4 – Full Employment, Excess Demand & Deficient Demand

1. Full Employment

Full Employment refers to a situation in which all those persons who are willing and able to work at the prevailing wage rate get employment, subject to the existence of normal frictional and structural unemployment.

Full employment does not necessarily mean zero unemployment.

2. Underemployment

Underemployment exists when people are willing and capable of working but cannot find sufficient employment opportunities.

An economy can have resources such as labour and capital that are not fully utilised.

3. Excess Demand

Excess Demand refers to a situation where aggregate demand is greater than the level of aggregate demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium.
Excess Demand → AD > AS at Full Employment

Excess demand creates upward pressure on the general price level and may result in inflationary pressure.

4. Inflationary Gap

Inflationary Gap is the excess of planned aggregate demand over the level of aggregate demand required to establish full employment.
Inflationary Gap = Excess Aggregate Demand

It indicates the amount by which aggregate demand needs to be reduced to eliminate excess demand at full employment.

5. Causes of Excess Demand

  • Increase in government expenditure.
  • Reduction in taxes.
  • Increase in money supply.
  • Increase in private investment.
  • Increase in consumption expenditure.
  • Increase in exports.
  • Increase in consumer confidence.

6. Effects of Excess Demand

  • Creates inflationary pressure.
  • Raises the general price level.
  • May increase demand for scarce resources.
  • Can create pressure on wages and costs.
  • May reduce the purchasing power of money.

7. Measures to Correct Excess Demand

The government and central bank can adopt contractionary measures to reduce aggregate demand.

Fiscal Measures

  • Decrease government expenditure: Reduces aggregate demand directly.
  • Increase taxes: Reduces disposable income and consumption expenditure.
  • Increase public borrowing: Can help reduce purchasing power from the economy.

Monetary Measures

  • Increase bank rate.
  • Increase repo rate.
  • Increase cash reserve requirements.
  • Increase statutory liquidity requirements.
  • Sell government securities through open market operations.

8. Deficient Demand

Deficient Demand refers to a situation where aggregate demand is less than the level of aggregate demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium.
Deficient Demand → AD < Full Employment Level of AD

Deficient demand may lead to unemployment and a fall in the level of output and income.

9. Deflationary Gap

Deflationary Gap is the amount by which aggregate demand falls short of the aggregate demand required to achieve full employment.

It represents the deficiency in aggregate demand at the full-employment level.

Deflationary Gap = Required AD − Actual AD

10. Causes of Deficient Demand

  • Decrease in government expenditure.
  • Increase in taxes.
  • Decrease in private investment.
  • Decrease in money supply.
  • Decrease in consumption expenditure.
  • Decrease in exports.
  • Fall in business confidence.

11. Effects of Deficient Demand

  • Fall in output.
  • Fall in employment.
  • Increase in unemployment.
  • Reduction in income.
  • Low utilisation of productive resources.
  • Reduction in investment due to weak demand.

12. Measures to Correct Deficient Demand

Fiscal Measures

  • Increase government expenditure to raise aggregate demand.
  • Decrease taxes to increase disposable income and consumption.
  • Reduce public borrowing to increase purchasing power in the economy.

Monetary Measures

  • Decrease bank rate.
  • Decrease repo rate.
  • Decrease cash reserve requirements.
  • Decrease statutory liquidity requirements.
  • Purchase government securities through open market operations.

13. Excess Demand vs Deficient Demand

Basis Excess Demand Deficient Demand
Meaning AD is higher than the required level at full employment. AD is lower than the required level at full employment.
Main problem Inflationary pressure Unemployment and recessionary pressure
Output Demand may exceed available output at full employment. Output remains below the full-employment level.
Fiscal policy Decrease government expenditure and/or increase taxes. Increase government expenditure and/or decrease taxes.
Monetary policy Restrictive/contractionary monetary policy. Expansionary monetary policy.

14. Quick Policy Comparison

Situation Government Action Central Bank Action
Excess Demand Reduce expenditure / Increase taxes Reduce money supply / Raise interest rates
Deficient Demand Increase expenditure / Reduce taxes Increase money supply / Lower interest rates

15. Important Monetary Policy Instruments

Instrument When Excess Demand Exists When Deficient Demand Exists
Bank Rate Increase Decrease
Repo Rate Increase Decrease
CRR Increase Decrease
SLR Increase Decrease
Open Market Operations Sell securities Buy securities
⚠️ Remember the Direction:

Excess Demand → Reduce AD
Deficient Demand → Increase AD

This simple relationship helps in remembering most corrective measures.
⭐ Board Exam Points – Section 4
  • Excess demand creates inflationary pressure.
  • Deficient demand creates unemployment and recessionary pressure.
  • Inflationary gap is associated with excess demand.
  • Deflationary gap is associated with deficient demand.
  • To correct excess demand, contractionary fiscal and monetary policies are used.
  • To correct deficient demand, expansionary fiscal and monetary policies are used.
  • Increase in government expenditure increases aggregate demand.
  • Increase in taxes reduces aggregate demand.
  • Purchase of securities by the central bank increases liquidity.
  • Sale of securities by the central bank reduces liquidity.
