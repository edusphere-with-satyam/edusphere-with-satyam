📘 SECTION 1 — Aggregate Demand, Consumption & Saving

Complete conceptual foundation of the unit

🎯 Learning Objectives

Understand the meaning and components of Aggregate Demand.
Understand Consumption Function and Saving Function.
Understand APC, MPC, APS and MPS.
Understand the relationship between consumption and saving.

1. Aggregate Demand

Aggregate Demand (AD) refers to the total planned expenditure on goods and services in an economy during a given period.

In a simple two-sector economy consisting of households and firms, aggregate demand consists of expenditure on consumption and investment.

AD = C + I

Where:

C = Consumption expenditure
I = Investment expenditure

2. Components of Aggregate Demand

1. Consumption Expenditure (C): Expenditure by households on consumption goods and services.
2. Investment Expenditure (I): Expenditure by firms on capital goods such as machinery, equipment and inventories.
In the simple two-sector model:

AD = C + I

3. Consumption Function

Consumption function shows the functional relationship between consumption expenditure and income.
C = C̄ + bY

Where:

C̄ = Autonomous consumption
b = Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)
Y = Income
Autonomous Consumption: Consumption which takes place even when income is zero.

Induced Consumption: Consumption which changes with the level of income.

4. Average Propensity to Consume (APC)

APC shows the ratio of consumption expenditure to income.
APC = C / Y

APC can be expressed as a percentage by multiplying the result by 100.

APC can be greater than 1 when consumption is greater than income.
APC = 1 when consumption equals income.
APC is generally greater than MPC when autonomous consumption is positive.

5. Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)

MPC measures the change in consumption due to a change in income.
MPC = ΔC / ΔY
In the simple model, MPC lies between 0 and 1.
A higher MPC means a larger portion of additional income is spent on consumption.

6. Saving Function

Saving is the part of income which is not spent on consumption.
S = Y − C
If income is ₹1,000 and consumption is ₹800:

Saving = ₹1,000 − ₹800 = ₹200

7. Average Propensity to Save (APS)

APS = S / Y

APS shows the proportion of income saved.

8. Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)

MPS = ΔS / ΔY

MPS shows the change in saving resulting from a change in income.

9. Important Relationships

APC + APS = 1
MPC + MPS = 1
Therefore:

MPC = 1 − MPS

MPS = 1 − MPC

APC = 1 − APS

APS = 1 − APC

⚡ SECTION 1 BOOSTER

✔ AD = C + I

✔ Saving = Income − Consumption

✔ APC = C/Y

✔ MPC = ΔC/ΔY

✔ APS = S/Y

✔ MPS = ΔS/ΔY

✔ MPC + MPS = 1

✔ APC + APS = 1

📊 SECTION 2 — Equilibrium Income & Investment Multiplier

Most important numerical and analytical portion

10. Equilibrium Level of Income

Equilibrium level of income is the level at which planned aggregate demand is equal to planned output.
AD = AS

In the simple two-sector model:

Y = C + I

At equilibrium, there is no tendency for producers to increase or decrease output.

11. Equilibrium Through Saving-Investment Approach

Another way of determining equilibrium income is through the equality of planned saving and planned investment.

S = I
At equilibrium: Planned Saving = Planned Investment

12. Numerical Example — Equilibrium Income

Suppose:

Consumption function: C = 100 + 0.8Y
Investment: I = 100

At equilibrium:

Y = C + I

Y = 100 + 0.8Y + 100

Y − 0.8Y = 200

0.2Y = 200

Y = ₹1,000

13. Investment Multiplier

Investment multiplier shows the change in income resulting from a change in autonomous investment.
K = ΔY / ΔI

In a simple economy:

K = 1 / (1 − MPC)
K = 1 / MPS

14. Numerical Example — Multiplier

If MPC = 0.8:

K = 1 / (1 − 0.8)

K = 1 / 0.2

K = 5

If investment increases by ₹100 crore:

ΔY = K × ΔI

ΔY = 5 × 100

ΔY = ₹500 crore

15. Important Multiplier Relationships

MPC MPS Multiplier
0.5 0.5 2
0.6 0.4 2.5
0.75 0.25 4
0.8 0.2 5
0.9 0.1 10
Higher MPC → Higher Multiplier
Higher MPS → Lower Multiplier

16. Investment Multiplier Process

1️⃣ Initial increase in investment creates additional income.
2️⃣ Recipients spend a part of this additional income.
3️⃣ This expenditure becomes income for others.
4️⃣ They again spend a part of their additional income.
5️⃣ The process continues in successive rounds.
Therefore, an initial change in investment causes a multiple change in income.

🧮 Numerical Booster

Question: MPC = 0.75 and investment increases by ₹200 crore. Find change in income.

MPS = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25

K = 1/0.25 = 4

ΔY = 4 × 200

Answer = ₹800 crore

🚨 Common Numerical Mistakes

❌ Do not use MPC directly as the multiplier.

❌ Do not confuse ΔY with ΔI.

❌ Remember: MPS = 1 − MPC.

❌ Multiplier = 1/MPS = 1/(1−MPC).

❌ At equilibrium, AD = AS or S = I.

📈 SECTION 3 — Full Employment & Excess Demand

Meaning, causes, effects and correction

17. Full Employment

Full employment refers to a situation in which all those who are willing and able to work at the prevailing wage rate are employed, subject to the existence of normal frictional and structural unemployment.
Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.

18. Involuntary Unemployment

Involuntary unemployment occurs when people are willing and able to work at the prevailing wage rate but cannot find employment.

19. Excess Demand

Excess demand occurs when aggregate demand is greater than the level of aggregate supply corresponding to full employment.
Excess Demand = AD − AS at Full Employment
Excess demand creates an inflationary gap.

20. Causes of Excess Demand

Increase in government expenditure.
Reduction in taxes.
Increase in money supply.
Increase in private investment.
Increase in consumption expenditure.
Increase in exports.

21. Effects of Excess Demand

Prices tend to rise.
Inflationary pressure increases.
Demand exceeds productive capacity at full employment.
There is an inflationary gap.

22. Measures to Correct Excess Demand

Monetary Measures

Increase Bank Rate.
Increase Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR).
Increase Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR), where applicable.
Sell government securities through Open Market Operations.
These measures reduce credit availability and aggregate demand.

Fiscal Measures

Decrease government expenditure.
Increase taxes.
These measures reduce aggregate demand.

23. Easy Memory Trick

Excess Demand → Demand is TOO HIGH

Therefore:
⬇ Government Expenditure
⬆ Taxes
⬆ Interest/credit restrictions

Goal → Reduce Aggregate Demand

📉 SECTION 4 — Deficient Demand + Complete Exam Booster

Correction measures, formulas, MCQs and final revision

24. Deficient Demand

Deficient demand occurs when aggregate demand is less than the level of aggregate supply corresponding to full employment.
Deficient demand creates a deflationary gap.

25. Causes of Deficient Demand

Decrease in government expenditure.
Increase in taxes.
Decrease in money supply.
Decrease in private investment.
Decrease in consumption expenditure.
Decrease in exports.

26. Effects of Deficient Demand

Output and income may fall.
Employment may decline.
Unemployment may increase.
Deflationary pressure may arise.

27. Measures to Correct Deficient Demand

Fiscal Measures

Increase government expenditure.
Reduce taxes.
These measures increase aggregate demand.

Monetary Measures

Reduce Bank Rate.
Reduce CRR.
Reduce SLR, where applicable.
Purchase government securities through Open Market Operations.
These measures increase credit availability and aggregate demand.

📌 COMPLETE UNIT SUMMARY

The determination of income and employment explains how the equilibrium level of income is determined by aggregate demand and aggregate supply. In a simple two-sector economy, aggregate demand consists of consumption and investment expenditure.

Consumption and saving are related to income through their respective functions. APC, MPC, APS and MPS help us understand how income is divided between consumption and saving.

Equilibrium income is achieved when planned aggregate demand equals planned output. It can also be understood through the equality of planned saving and planned investment.

The investment multiplier explains how a change in autonomous investment can cause a multiple change in income.

When aggregate demand is higher than the required level at full employment, there is excess demand and an inflationary gap. When aggregate demand is lower, there is deficient demand and a deflationary gap. Fiscal and monetary measures are used to correct these situations.
