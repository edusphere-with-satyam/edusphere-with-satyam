💡 Introduction to Cost, Revenue and Producer’s Equilibrium

A producer uses various inputs such as labour, raw materials, machines and buildings to produce goods and services. Production involves expenditure on these inputs, which is called cost. The producer earns money by selling the output, which is called revenue.

A producer must compare cost with revenue while deciding the level of output. The level of output at which the producer is able to maximise profit is called producer’s equilibrium.

In simple words: A producer asks three basic questions: How much does production cost? How much revenue will the output generate? And at what level of output should production be carried out?

💰 Cost of Production

Formal Definition: Cost of production refers to the expenditure incurred by a producer on various factors of production and other inputs used for producing a commodity.
Simple Definition: Cost is the money spent by a producer to produce goods or services.

For example, a producer may spend money on wages, raw materials, electricity, rent, transport and machinery. All such expenditures form part of the cost of production.

Economic Cost and Money Cost

Money cost refers to the expenditure measured in monetary terms. Economic cost is a broader concept that considers the economic sacrifice involved in using resources for production, including relevant opportunity costs.

For the standard Class 11 numerical treatment in this topic, the focus is mainly on the short-run cost concepts such as TFC, TVC, TC, AFC, AVC, AC and MC.

🏢 Fixed Cost

Formal Definition: Fixed cost is the cost that does not change with the level of output in the short run.
Simple Definition: Whether the producer produces more, less or even zero output, fixed cost remains unchanged in the short run.

Examples include rent of a factory, insurance and certain fixed contractual payments.

Key point: TFC remains constant at every level of output in the short run.

🔄 Variable Cost

Formal Definition: Variable cost is the cost that changes with the level of output in the short run.
Simple Definition: When production increases, variable cost generally increases; when production falls, variable cost generally falls.

Examples include raw materials, power used according to production and wages of workers whose employment varies with output.

➕ Total Cost

Formal Definition: Total cost is the total expenditure incurred by a producer on producing a given quantity of output.
TC = TFC + TVC

Since total cost consists of both fixed and variable costs, a change in TVC changes TC while TFC remains constant in the short run.

📈 Relationship among TFC, TVC and TC

📊 View Graph and Detailed Interpretation

Graph Interpretation

TFC: The horizontal TFC curve shows that fixed cost remains constant regardless of the level of output in the short run.

TVC: TVC rises as output increases. Its shape reflects the changing productivity of the variable factor. When the variable factor becomes more productive initially, TVC may rise at a slower rate; when diminishing returns set in, TVC rises at an increasing rate.

TC: Since TC = TFC + TVC, the TC curve has the same general shape as TVC but lies above it by the amount of TFC.

Important relationship: The vertical distance between TC and TVC remains equal to TFC at every level of output.

📐 Average Cost

Formal Definition: Average cost is the cost per unit of output produced.
AC = TC ÷ Q

Average cost can also be divided into average fixed cost and average variable cost.

AC = AFC + AVC

📉 Average Fixed Cost

Formal Definition: Average fixed cost is fixed cost per unit of output.
AFC = TFC ÷ Q

As output increases, the same fixed cost is distributed over a larger number of units. Therefore, AFC continuously falls.

Example: If fixed cost is ₹100, AFC is ₹100 at one unit, ₹50 at two units and ₹25 at four units.

📉 Average Variable Cost

Formal Definition: Average variable cost is variable cost per unit of output.
AVC = TVC ÷ Q

AVC generally falls initially because productivity of the variable factor improves. After a point, diminishing returns cause AVC to rise. Therefore, the AVC curve is generally U-shaped.

⚡ Marginal Cost

Formal Definition: Marginal cost is the addition to total cost caused by producing one additional unit of output.
MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ

Because fixed cost does not change with output in the short run, the change in total cost is caused by the change in variable cost. Therefore:

MC = ΔTVC ÷ ΔQ
Simple meaning: MC tells us the extra cost of producing one more unit.

📊 Relationship among AFC, AVC, AC and MC

📈 View Cost Curves and Detailed Interpretation

Graph Interpretation

AFC: AFC continuously falls because a fixed amount of cost is spread over increasingly more units of output. It approaches the horizontal axis but does not normally touch it.

AVC: AVC is generally U-shaped. It initially falls because the variable factor becomes more productive. After diminishing returns begin, AVC rises.

AC: AC is also generally U-shaped. It is the sum of AFC and AVC. At first, falling AFC and falling AVC reduce AC. Later, rising AVC becomes strong enough to increase AC.

MC: MC generally falls initially and rises later because of changes in marginal productivity. It cuts AVC and AC at their respective minimum points.

Exam rule: MC intersects AVC and AC at the minimum points of AVC and AC respectively. This is one of the most important relationships in short-run cost analysis.

💵 Revenue

Formal Definition: Revenue refers to the receipts obtained by a producer from the sale of output.
Simple Definition: Revenue is the money a producer receives by selling goods or services.

The three important revenue concepts are: Total Revenue (TR), Average Revenue (AR), and Marginal Revenue (MR).

💰 Total Revenue (TR)

Formal Definition: Total revenue is the total amount received by a producer from the sale of a given quantity of output.
TR = Price × Quantity Sold

For example, if a producer sells 10 units at ₹20 per unit:

TR = ₹20 × 10 = ₹200

💵 Average Revenue (AR)

Formal Definition: Average revenue is revenue earned per unit of output sold.
AR = TR ÷ Q

Since TR = P × Q:

AR = P
Therefore, AR is equal to price.

➕ Marginal Revenue (MR)

Formal Definition: Marginal revenue is the addition to total revenue resulting from the sale of one additional unit of output.
MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ
Simple meaning: MR tells us how much additional revenue is earned from selling one more unit.

🏪 Revenue under Perfect Competition

Under perfect competition, an individual firm is a price taker. The market determines the price and the individual firm accepts that price.

Therefore, each additional unit can be sold at the same price. As a result:

AR = MR = Price

📈 TR, AR and MR under Perfect Competition

📊 View Revenue Graph and Detailed Interpretation

Graph Interpretation

TR: Since price remains constant under perfect competition, every additional unit adds the same amount to total revenue. Therefore, TR rises at a constant rate and is represented by a straight upward-sloping line from the origin.

AR: AR equals price. Since price is constant, AR is represented by a horizontal line.

MR: MR also equals price because selling one additional unit adds exactly the same price to total revenue. Therefore, MR coincides with AR.

Key conclusion: Under perfect competition, the firm's demand curve, AR curve and MR curve are horizontal at the market price.

⚖️ Producer’s Equilibrium

Formal Definition: Producer’s equilibrium refers to the level of output at which a producer has no incentive to change the level of production because profit is maximised, subject to the relevant conditions.
Simple Definition: It is the output level at which the producer chooses to stop increasing or decreasing production because the chosen level gives the maximum profit under the given conditions.

MR–MC Approach

The MR–MC approach determines producer’s equilibrium by comparing marginal revenue with marginal cost.

MR = MC

At the equilibrium output, the revenue from one additional unit is equal to the cost of producing that additional unit.

📌 Why Must MC Cut MR from Below?

The condition MR = MC alone is not sufficient. The MC curve should be rising at the point of equality and should cut the MR curve from below.

Before the equilibrium point, if: MR > MC, producing an additional unit adds more to revenue than to cost. Therefore, increasing output is beneficial.

After the equilibrium point, if: MC > MR, producing an additional unit adds more to cost than to revenue. Therefore, increasing output reduces profit.

Thus, the producer reaches equilibrium where the incentive to expand production ends and the profit-maximising output is determined by the MR–MC condition.

📊 Producer’s Equilibrium: MR–MC Graph

📈 View Graph and Detailed Interpretation

Graph Interpretation

The horizontal MR line represents constant marginal revenue under perfect competition. The rising MC curve represents the additional cost of producing successive units.

At the equilibrium output Qe, MC becomes equal to MR.

To the left of Qe, MC is below MR. Hence, an additional unit adds more revenue than cost and the producer has an incentive to increase output.

To the right of Qe, MC is above MR. Hence, an additional unit adds more cost than revenue and increasing output reduces profit.

Therefore, the intersection at Qe identifies the profit-maximising output under the standard MR–MC approach.

Exam point: Equilibrium requires MR = MC and MC must be rising and cut MR from below.

🏭 Producer’s Equilibrium under Perfect Competition

Under perfect competition, the firm accepts the market price. Therefore:

AR = MR = P

The firm compares this constant MR with MC. The equilibrium quantity is the quantity at which:

MR = MC

with MC rising and cutting MR from below.

This means the producer continues expanding output as long as the additional revenue from another unit is greater than its additional cost. Once MC becomes greater than MR, further expansion is not desirable.

⚠️ Common Student Confusions

1. TFC vs TVC

TFC does not change with output in the short run, whereas TVC changes with output.

2. AC vs MC

AC is the average cost per unit, while MC is the additional cost of producing one more unit.

3. AR vs MR

AR is revenue per unit. MR is the additional revenue from selling one more unit. They are equal under perfect competition because price remains constant.

4. MR = MC does not mean MC is constant

MR = MC is an equality at the equilibrium output. It does not mean that the entire MC curve is equal to MR.

5. Why does MC cut AC at its minimum?

If MC is below AC, the average tends to fall. If MC is above AC, the average tends to rise. Therefore, MC meets AC at AC's minimum point. The same logic applies to AVC.

🧮 Five Strong Numerical Questions

Question 1: A firm has TFC of ₹500 and TVC of ₹1,200 at an output of 100 units. Calculate TC, AFC, AVC and AC.
View Answer
TC = TFC + TVC = ₹500 + ₹1,200 = ₹1,700 AFC = TFC ÷ Q = ₹500 ÷ 100 = ₹5 AVC = TVC ÷ Q = ₹1,200 ÷ 100 = ₹12 AC = TC ÷ Q = ₹1,700 ÷ 100 = ₹17 Check: AC = AFC + AVC = ₹5 + ₹12 = ₹17
Question 2: A firm's total cost rises from ₹800 to ₹950 when output increases from 40 units to 50 units. Calculate marginal cost.
View Answer
MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ Change in TC = ₹950 − ₹800 = ₹150 Change in Q = 50 − 40 = 10 units Therefore: MC = ₹150 ÷ 10 = ₹15 per unit
Question 3: A producer sells 80 units at ₹25 each. Calculate TR and AR.
View Answer
TR = P × Q = ₹25 × 80 = ₹2,000 AR = TR ÷ Q = ₹2,000 ÷ 80 = ₹25 Therefore: AR = Price = ₹25
Question 4: The total revenue of a firm increases from ₹2,000 to ₹2,300 when output increases from 100 units to 120 units. Calculate marginal revenue.
View Answer
MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ Change in TR = ₹2,300 − ₹2,000 = ₹300 Change in Q = 120 − 100 = 20 units Therefore: MR = ₹300 ÷ 20 = ₹15 per unit
Question 5: A firm has the following marginal revenue and marginal cost:
Output MR MC
1₹12₹7
2₹12₹9
3₹12₹12
4₹12₹15
5₹12₹19
Find the producer's equilibrium output using the MR–MC approach.
View Answer
At Q = 1: MR > MC → ₹12 > ₹7 So increasing output is beneficial. At Q = 2: MR > MC → ₹12 > ₹9 So increasing output is still beneficial. At Q = 3: MR = MC → ₹12 = ₹12 This is the equilibrium condition. At Q = 4: MC > MR → ₹15 > ₹12 Further increase in output reduces profit. Therefore: Producer's equilibrium output = 3 units. The condition is satisfied because MC has risen from ₹9 at Q = 2 to ₹12 at Q = 3 and then exceeds MR.
