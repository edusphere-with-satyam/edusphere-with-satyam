Introduction to Supply

In economics, production is undertaken by producers with the objective of supplying goods and services to the market. A producer does not supply a commodity randomly. The quantity supplied depends upon several economic factors such as the price of the commodity, cost of inputs, technology, taxes, subsidies, number of sellers and expectations about future prices.

The concept of supply helps us understand the producer's side of the market. While demand explains the behaviour of consumers, supply explains the behaviour of producers.

In simple words: Demand tells us how much consumers are willing to buy, whereas supply tells us how much producers are willing and able to sell at different prices.

The study of supply is important for understanding market equilibrium, price determination, production decisions and the effect of changes in market conditions.

Meaning of Supply

Formal Definition:
Supply refers to the quantity of a commodity that a producer is willing and able to offer for sale at different possible prices during a given period of time, assuming other factors remain constant.
Simple Definition:
Supply means the quantity of a commodity that a seller is ready and able to sell at a particular price during a particular period.

Important Elements of Supply

A proper definition of supply contains several important elements:

  1. Quantity: Supply refers to a specific quantity of a commodity.
  2. Willingness: The producer must be willing to sell the commodity.
  3. Ability: The producer must also have the ability to supply it.
  4. Price: Supply is generally studied at different prices.
  5. Time: Supply must relate to a particular period.
  6. Other factors: When we study the law of supply, other factors are assumed to remain constant.
Example:
If a shopkeeper is willing and able to sell 100 notebooks at ₹50 per notebook during one week, then 100 notebooks represent the quantity supplied at that price and during that period.
Exam Point: “Willingness to sell” alone is not enough. A producer must also have the ability to supply the commodity.

Stock and Supply

Stock and supply are related concepts, but they are not the same. Students often use these terms interchangeably, which is incorrect.

Stock: Stock refers to the total quantity of a commodity available with a producer at a particular point of time.
Supply: Supply refers to the quantity of a commodity that the producer is willing and able to offer for sale at a particular price during a particular period.
Basis Stock Supply
Meaning Total quantity available Quantity offered for sale
Price relationship Not necessarily related to a particular price Always considered in relation to price
Time Usually refers to quantity available at a point of time Refers to quantity offered during a period
Relationship Stock can be greater than supply Supply can never exceed available stock
Example:
A farmer has 1,000 kg of wheat in stock. At the current market price, he may decide to sell only 600 kg. Therefore, stock = 1,000 kg, while supply = 600 kg.
Remember: Stock is the quantity available, while supply is the quantity actually offered for sale.

Individual Supply

Meaning: Individual supply refers to the quantity of a commodity supplied by an individual producer or seller at different prices during a given period.
Simple Meaning: It tells us how much one particular seller is willing and able to sell at different prices.
Example:
Suppose a baker supplies 20, 40, 60 and 80 cakes when the price is ₹100, ₹150, ₹200 and ₹250 respectively. This is the individual supply of that baker.

Market Supply

Meaning: Market supply refers to the total quantity of a commodity supplied by all producers or sellers in the market at different prices during a given period.

Market supply is obtained by adding the quantities supplied by all individual producers at the same price.

Market Supply = Sum of Individual Supplies

Example

Price (₹) Seller A Seller B Market Supply
10 20 30 50
20 30 40 70
30 40 50 90
40 50 60 110
50 60 70 130

At ₹30, Seller A supplies 40 units and Seller B supplies 50 units.

Market Supply = 40 + 50 = 90 units
Thus, market supply is obtained by horizontal addition of individual supplies at each price.

Determinants of Supply

The quantity supplied of a commodity is affected by several factors. Price is important, but it is not the only factor affecting supply.

1. Price of the Commodity

Other things remaining constant, a rise in the price of a commodity generally encourages producers to supply more because higher prices may increase profitability.

2. Prices of Inputs

Inputs are resources used in production, such as raw materials, labour, electricity, fuel and machinery services.

If input prices rise, the cost of production increases. This may reduce the producer's willingness to supply the commodity.

3. Technology

Improved technology can increase productivity and reduce the cost of production. Therefore, producers may be able to supply more at the same price.

4. Taxes

Taxes imposed on production or sale can increase the cost of supplying a commodity. Other things remaining constant, higher taxes tend to reduce supply.

5. Subsidies

A subsidy is financial assistance provided by the government. A subsidy may reduce the effective cost of production and encourage producers to increase supply.

6. Number of Firms

If more firms enter a market, total market supply generally increases. If firms leave the market, market supply may decrease.

7. Expectations About Future Prices

Producers may consider expected future prices while deciding how much to supply today.

For example, if producers expect prices to rise significantly in the future, they may hold back some stock from the present market.

8. Goals of the Firm

Traditional economic analysis often assumes profit maximisation. However, firms may also have other objectives such as increasing market share, maintaining sales or ensuring long-term growth.

9. Natural and Environmental Conditions

In agriculture and some natural-resource industries, weather, rainfall, climate and other natural conditions can strongly affect supply.

Important: A change in the price of the commodity generally causes a movement along the same supply curve, whereas changes in other determinants generally cause a shift of the supply curve.

Why Does the Supply Curve Generally Slope Upward?

The supply curve generally slopes upward because producers are normally willing to supply a larger quantity when they receive a higher price, other things remaining constant.

Reason 1: Profit Incentive

A higher price can increase the potential profit from selling a commodity. This encourages producers to expand production and supply.

Reason 2: Rising Marginal Cost

In the short run, some factors of production are fixed. As production is expanded, additional units may require increasingly costly use of variable factors. Therefore, producers generally need a higher price to supply larger quantities.

Reason 3: Entry of Producers

A higher market price can make production more attractive. In some markets, additional producers may enter, increasing total supply.

The upward slope represents a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied, not that every individual supply curve must always be upward sloping under every possible circumstance.

Slope of Supply Curve

The slope of a supply curve indicates how much the price changes when quantity supplied changes by one unit, depending upon which variables are placed on the axes.

Since the standard diagram places price on the Y-axis and quantity supplied on the X-axis:

Slope of Supply Curve = Change in Price ÷ Change in Quantity Supplied

For the numerical supply schedule above, price and quantity both increase in the same direction. Therefore, the curve slopes upward.

Do not confuse slope with elasticity. Slope measures the rate of change between the variables, whereas elasticity measures the degree of responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.

Movement Along the Supply Curve

When the price of the commodity changes and all other factors remain constant, the quantity supplied changes along the same supply curve.

This is called movement along the supply curve.

There are two forms:

  • Expansion of supply
  • Contraction of supply

Expansion of Supply

Expansion of supply refers to an increase in quantity supplied due to a rise in the price of the commodity, other things remaining constant.

Contraction of Supply

Contraction of supply refers to a decrease in quantity supplied due to a fall in the price of the commodity, other things remaining constant.
Expansion of Supply – Movement Upward Along the Same Curve
Interpretation:

The producer moves from point A to point B on the same supply curve S.

Price rises and quantity supplied also rises. Since the supply curve itself has not shifted, this is an expansion of supply, not an increase in supply.

Exam trap: Expansion/contraction = movement along the same supply curve due to a change in price.

Shift in Supply Curve

A shift in supply occurs when supply changes because of factors other than the price of the commodity, while the price is held constant for comparison.

A shift can be either:

  • Increase in supply
  • Decrease in supply

Increase in Supply

An increase in supply occurs when producers are willing and able to supply more at the same price due to favourable changes in factors other than the commodity's own price.

Examples:

  • Improvement in technology
  • Fall in input prices
  • Reduction in taxes
  • Increase in subsidies
  • Entry of new firms

Decrease in Supply

A decrease in supply occurs when producers are willing and able to supply less at the same price due to an unfavourable change in factors other than the commodity's own price.

Examples:

  • Rise in input prices
  • Increase in taxes
  • Removal or reduction of subsidies
  • Adverse weather conditions
  • Exit of firms
Increase in Supply – Rightward Shift
Graph Interpretation:

The original supply curve is S₁. The new supply curve S₂ lies to the right of S₁.

At the same price, the producer supplies a greater quantity after the shift. For example, at ₹30, quantity supplied increases from 30 units to 40 units.

The cause is not a change in the commodity's own price. Instead, some other determinant of supply has changed favourably, such as technology, input prices, taxes or subsidies.

How to Draw:
  1. Draw the original upward-sloping supply curve and label it S₁.
  2. Draw another upward-sloping curve to its right and label it S₂.
  3. Mark numerical values on both axes.
  4. Take one common price and draw a horizontal guide.
  5. Show that quantity supplied is greater on S₂ at the same price.
  6. Write: Increase in Supply → Rightward Shift.

Movement Along Supply Curve vs Shift in Supply Curve

Basis Movement Along Supply Curve Shift in Supply Curve
Cause Change in price of the commodity Change in other determinants
Curve Same curve New supply curve
Forms Expansion and contraction Increase and decrease
Graph Movement from one point to another Entire curve shifts
Example Price rises from ₹20 to ₹30 Technology improves
One-line memory rule:
Price changes → Movement.
Other factors change → Shift.

Decrease in Supply – Leftward Shift

A decrease in supply means that producers supply less at every given price because of an unfavourable change in one or more determinants of supply.

Example:
Suppose a rise in the price of raw materials increases production costs. At ₹30, producers may now supply only 20 units instead of 30 units. The supply curve shifts leftward.
Important: A decrease in supply is different from contraction of supply. A decrease shifts the entire supply curve to the left, while contraction is movement downward along the same curve because of a fall in price.

Law of Supply

Statement: Other things remaining constant, there is a direct relationship between the price of a commodity and its quantity supplied. As price rises, quantity supplied generally rises; as price falls, quantity supplied generally falls.
Simple Explanation: Producers generally want to sell more when they can receive a higher price and less when they receive a lower price.

Assumptions of the Law of Supply

The law of supply assumes that factors other than the commodity's own price remain constant. These include:

  • Prices of inputs
  • Technology
  • Taxes and subsidies
  • Number of firms
  • Future price expectations
  • Natural conditions
The law explains a movement along a supply curve. A change in these other factors causes the supply curve itself to shift.

Price Elasticity of Supply

Formal Definition:
Price elasticity of supply measures the degree of responsiveness of quantity supplied of a commodity to a change in its price, other things remaining constant.
Simple Meaning:
It tells us how strongly quantity supplied responds when price changes.

Two commodities may experience the same percentage change in price but show different percentage changes in quantity supplied. Price elasticity of supply helps us measure this difference in responsiveness.

Es = Percentage Change in Quantity Supplied ÷ Percentage Change in Price

Where:

  • Es = Price elasticity of supply
  • Percentage change in quantity supplied measures the change in quantity supplied relative to the original quantity.
  • Percentage change in price measures the change in price relative to the original price.

Measurement of Price Elasticity of Supply by Percentage-Change Method

Under the percentage-change method, elasticity is calculated by dividing the percentage change in quantity supplied by the percentage change in price.

Es = (% Change in Quantity Supplied) / (% Change in Price)

Percentage Change

Percentage Change = (Change ÷ Original Value) × 100

Therefore:

Es = [(ΔQ ÷ Q) × 100] ÷ [(ΔP ÷ P) × 100]

The 100 cancels out, giving the useful form:

Es = (ΔQ / Q) × (P / ΔP)
For Class 11 numerical questions using the percentage-change method, always identify the original price and original quantity before calculating percentage changes.

Degrees of Price Elasticity of Supply

Price elasticity of supply can take different values depending upon the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price.

1. Perfectly Inelastic Supply

When quantity supplied does not change at all despite a change in price, supply is perfectly inelastic.

Es = 0

The supply curve is vertical.

2. Relatively Inelastic Supply

When the percentage change in quantity supplied is less than the percentage change in price, supply is relatively inelastic.

0 < Es < 1

3. Unitary Elastic Supply

When the percentage change in quantity supplied is equal to the percentage change in price, supply is unitary elastic.

Es = 1

4. Relatively Elastic Supply

When the percentage change in quantity supplied is greater than the percentage change in price, supply is relatively elastic.

Es > 1

5. Perfectly Elastic Supply

When an extremely small change in price can be associated with an unlimited change in quantity supplied at a given price, supply is perfectly elastic in the standard theoretical representation.

Es = ∞
Value of Es Degree Meaning
0 Perfectly Inelastic Quantity supplied does not respond
Between 0 and 1 Relatively Inelastic Quantity responds less than price
1 Unitary Elastic Quantity and price change proportionately
Greater than 1 Relatively Elastic Quantity responds more than price
∞ Perfectly Elastic Extremely high responsiveness

Graphical Understanding of Elasticity of Supply

The shape of the supply curve gives a visual indication of how strongly quantity supplied responds to price changes. However, students should remember that elasticity is a numerical concept and cannot always be identified simply from the visual steepness of a curve without considering the scale and the particular method of measurement.

Perfectly Inelastic Supply
Interpretation:

The vertical supply curve shows that quantity supplied remains fixed even when price changes.

Therefore, the percentage change in quantity supplied is zero and elasticity of supply is zero.

Perfectly Elastic Supply
Interpretation:

The horizontal supply curve represents perfectly elastic supply at a particular price.

The theoretical case indicates that suppliers are willing to supply different quantities at the given price, while a price below that level would not attract supply in the standard model.

Factors Affecting Price Elasticity of Supply

1. Time Period

Time is one of the most important determinants of elasticity of supply.

In a very short period, producers may not have enough time to increase production even when price rises. Therefore, supply tends to be relatively less elastic.

Over a longer period, producers have more time to adjust production, purchase machinery, hire workers and expand capacity. Therefore, supply can become more elastic.

2. Spare Production Capacity

If firms have unused or spare capacity, they can increase production quickly when price rises. Supply is therefore likely to be more elastic.

3. Nature of the Commodity

Some goods can be produced more easily than others. Goods that can be produced using flexible production processes may have more elastic supply.

4. Mobility of Factors of Production

If labour, capital and other factors can easily move from one use to another, producers can respond more quickly to price changes.

5. Storability of the Commodity

Goods that can be stored for a considerable period can often be supplied more flexibly because producers can release stored stocks when market conditions change.

6. Availability of Inputs

If raw materials and other inputs are readily available, production can be increased more easily. This tends to make supply more elastic.

Core idea: The easier and quicker it is for producers to adjust quantity supplied, the more elastic supply tends to be.

Time Period and Elasticity of Supply

Suppose the price of a commodity suddenly increases. Producers cannot always increase output immediately.

Very Short Period

Production capacity is largely fixed. The producer may only be able to sell the existing stock or slightly adjust supply.

Shorter Adjustment Period

Some variable inputs can be changed, allowing producers to increase output to some extent.

Long Period

Firms can expand plant capacity, purchase machinery, hire additional workers and make other production adjustments. Therefore, supply is generally more responsive.

Memory Rule:
More time for adjustment → Greater ability to respond → Generally more elastic supply.

Worked Example: Calculating Price Elasticity of Supply

Suppose the price of a commodity rises from ₹20 to ₹25 and quantity supplied rises from 100 units to 125 units.

Step 1: Calculate Change in Quantity

Change in quantity = 125 − 100 = 25 units

Step 2: Calculate Percentage Change in Quantity

(25 ÷ 100) × 100 = 25%

Step 3: Calculate Change in Price

Change in price = ₹25 − ₹20 = ₹5

Step 4: Calculate Percentage Change in Price

(5 ÷ 20) × 100 = 25%

Step 5: Calculate Elasticity

Es = 25% ÷ 25% = 1

Therefore, supply is unitary elastic.

Market Supply: Graphical Understanding

Market supply is obtained by adding the quantities supplied by all sellers at each price. The resulting quantities are then plotted against price to obtain the market supply curve.

Suppose:
At ₹20, Seller A supplies 20 units and Seller B supplies 30 units.
Therefore, market supply = 20 + 30 = 50 units.

The same process is repeated for every price. The resulting market supply schedule can then be represented graphically.

Exam Procedure:
  1. Prepare individual supply schedules.
  2. Take one price at a time.
  3. Add quantities supplied by all sellers.
  4. Prepare the market supply schedule.
  5. Plot price on Y-axis and market quantity supplied on X-axis.
  6. Join the plotted points to obtain the market supply curve.

Common Student Confusions and Exam Traps

Confusion 1: Stock vs Supply

Stock is the total quantity available. Supply is the quantity offered for sale at a particular price during a period.

Confusion 2: Expansion vs Increase in Supply

Expansion occurs because the commodity's own price rises. Increase in supply occurs because other determinants change favourably.

Confusion 3: Contraction vs Decrease in Supply

Contraction is movement down the same supply curve due to a fall in price. Decrease is a leftward shift caused by other factors.

Confusion 4: Individual Supply vs Market Supply

Individual supply belongs to one producer. Market supply is the total supply of all producers.

Confusion 5: Slope vs Elasticity

Slope measures the rate of change represented by the graph. Elasticity measures responsiveness in percentage terms.

Confusion 6: Price Change and Curve Shift

A change in the commodity's own price does not shift the supply curve in the standard supply analysis. It causes movement along the existing curve.

Very important for exams:
If the question says “price of the commodity changes”, think movement.
If the question says “technology, input price, tax, subsidy, number of firms etc. changes”, think shift.

Practice Numericals with Solutions

These questions are designed to cover the important numerical patterns from supply and price elasticity of supply.

Numerical 1 – Percentage Change Method

The price of a commodity rises from ₹40 to ₹50 and its quantity supplied rises from 200 units to 250 units. Calculate price elasticity of supply.

Solution:

Change in quantity = 250 − 200 = 50 units

Percentage change in quantity:

(50 ÷ 200) × 100 = 25%

Change in price = ₹50 − ₹40 = ₹10

Percentage change in price:

(10 ÷ 40) × 100 = 25%
Es = 25 ÷ 25 = 1

Answer: Es = 1, therefore supply is unitary elastic.

Numerical 2 – Relatively Elastic Supply

The price of a commodity increases from ₹50 to ₹60 and quantity supplied increases from 100 units to 140 units. Calculate price elasticity of supply.

Solution:

Change in quantity = 140 − 100 = 40 units

Percentage change in quantity = (40 ÷ 100) × 100 = 40%

Change in price = ₹60 − ₹50 = ₹10

Percentage change in price = (10 ÷ 50) × 100 = 20%
Es = 40 ÷ 20 = 2

Answer: Es = 2.

Since Es is greater than 1, supply is relatively elastic.

Numerical 3 – Relatively Inelastic Supply

Price rises from ₹100 to ₹120 while quantity supplied increases from 500 units to 550 units. Find price elasticity of supply.

Solution:

Change in quantity = 550 − 500 = 50 units

Percentage change in quantity = (50 ÷ 500) × 100 = 10%

Change in price = ₹120 − ₹100 = ₹20

Percentage change in price = (20 ÷ 100) × 100 = 20%
Es = 10 ÷ 20 = 0.5

Answer: Es = 0.5.

Since Es is between 0 and 1, supply is relatively inelastic.

Numerical 4 – Finding Percentage Change in Quantity

Price increases from ₹80 to ₹100. The price elasticity of supply is 1.5. If the original quantity supplied is 200 units, find the new quantity supplied.

Solution:

First calculate percentage change in price:

[(100 − 80) ÷ 80] × 100 = 25%

We know:

Es = % Change in Quantity ÷ % Change in Price

Therefore:

1.5 = % Change in Quantity ÷ 25
% Change in Quantity = 1.5 × 25 = 37.5%

Original quantity = 200 units.

Increase in quantity:

37.5% of 200 = 75 units

New quantity:

200 + 75 = 275 units

Answer: New quantity supplied = 275 units.

Numerical 5 – Finding Price Elasticity from Percentage Changes

The quantity supplied of a commodity increases by 15% when its price increases by 10%. Calculate price elasticity of supply and state its degree.

Solution:

Es = 15% ÷ 10%
Es = 1.5

Since Es is greater than 1, supply is relatively elastic.

Answer: Es = 1.5; supply is relatively elastic.

Important Questions

What is supply?

Supply refers to the quantity of a commodity that a producer is willing and able to offer for sale at different possible prices during a given period, other things remaining constant.

Differentiate between stock and supply.

Stock is the total quantity of a commodity available with a producer at a particular point of time, whereas supply is the quantity that the producer is willing and able to offer for sale at a particular price during a given period.

What is market supply?

Market supply is the total quantity of a commodity supplied by all producers in the market at different prices during a given period.

What is expansion of supply?

Expansion of supply is an increase in quantity supplied caused by a rise in the price of the commodity, represented by an upward movement along the same supply curve.

What is increase in supply?

Increase in supply means that more quantity is supplied at the same price because of favourable changes in determinants other than the commodity's own price. It is represented by a rightward shift of the supply curve.

What is price elasticity of supply?

Price elasticity of supply measures the degree of responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in the price of a commodity, other things remaining constant.

Application-Based Understanding

A new technology reduces the cost of producing smartphones. What happens to supply?

The cost of production falls. Producers can supply more smartphones at the same price. Therefore, supply increases and the supply curve shifts to the right.

The price of wheat rises. What happens to quantity supplied?

Other things remaining constant, producers generally increase quantity supplied. This is an expansion of supply and is represented by movement upward along the same supply curve.

The government increases a tax on production. What happens to supply?

The cost of production increases. Producers may supply less at the same price. Therefore, supply decreases and the supply curve shifts leftward.

A farmer expects the price of wheat to rise substantially next month. How may this affect present supply?

The farmer may hold back some stock from the current market in expectation of receiving a higher future price. Thus, present supply may decrease.

CBSE PYQ-Based Practice

Question 1

Distinguish between expansion of supply and increase in supply.

Answer: Expansion of supply is movement along the same supply curve due to a rise in the commodity's price. Increase in supply is a rightward shift of the supply curve due to favourable changes in factors other than the commodity's own price.

Question 2

Explain any three determinants of supply.

Answer: Important determinants include input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, number of firms, expectations about future prices and natural conditions. A rise in input prices generally reduces supply, whereas improved technology generally increases supply.

Question 3

Explain the concept of price elasticity of supply and state its formula.

Answer: Price elasticity of supply measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. It is measured as the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price.
