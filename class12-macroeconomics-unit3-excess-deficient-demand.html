Excess Demand and Deficient Demand

Academic Definition:
Excess demand and deficient demand refer to situations in which Aggregate Demand differs from the level of Aggregate Demand required to maintain equilibrium at the full-employment level of output.
In Simple Words:
When people, firms and the government together want to spend more than what is required to purchase the full-employment level of output, there is excess demand. When they want to spend less, there is deficient demand.

Both concepts are studied in relation to the full-employment level of output. They are important because changes in Aggregate Demand can create inflationary or deflationary pressure in an economy.

Core Idea:
Excess Demand → Inflationary Pressure

Deficient Demand → Deflationary / Recessionary Pressure
9.1

Meaning of Excess Demand

Academic Definition:
Excess demand is the situation in which Aggregate Demand is greater than the Aggregate Demand required to purchase the full-employment level of output.
Simple Explanation:
Suppose the economy is already producing at the full-employment level. If total planned expenditure increases beyond the level required to purchase that output, the economy experiences excess demand.

Why does excess demand occur?

At full employment, productive resources are already being used to a very high degree. Therefore, if Aggregate Demand continues to rise beyond the level consistent with full-employment output, firms cannot increase real output proportionately in the short run.

The additional demand therefore puts upward pressure on the general price level.

Excess Demand = Actual AD − AD required at Full Employment
Important: Excess demand is measured with reference to the full-employment level, not merely by saying that demand is “high”.
9.2

Meaning of Deficient Demand

Academic Definition:
Deficient demand is the situation in which Aggregate Demand is less than the Aggregate Demand required to purchase the full-employment level of output.
Simple Explanation:
If the economy has the capacity to produce at full employment but total planned expenditure is too low to purchase that output, the economy experiences deficient demand.

When demand is insufficient, firms may find that they cannot sell the level of output they could potentially produce at full employment. As a result, production and employment may fall.

Deficient Demand = AD required at Full Employment − Actual AD
Key Point: Deficient demand is associated with a shortfall in Aggregate Demand relative to the level required for full-employment output.
9.3

Inflationary Gap

Meaning:
Inflationary gap refers to the excess of Aggregate Demand over the level of Aggregate Demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium.
Simple Explanation:
The inflationary gap shows how much additional demand exists beyond the demand needed to purchase the full-employment level of output.
Inflationary Gap = Actual AD − AD at Full Employment

How does an inflationary gap arise?

  1. The economy reaches the full-employment level.
  2. Aggregate Demand increases further.
  3. Real output cannot increase proportionately because resources are already fully employed.
  4. The additional demand creates upward pressure on prices.
  5. This situation is called an inflationary gap.
Inflationary Gap
9.4

Deflationary Gap

Meaning:
Deflationary gap refers to the shortfall of Aggregate Demand from the level of Aggregate Demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium.
Simple Explanation:
When total spending in the economy is not sufficient to purchase the full-employment level of output, there is a deficiency in demand. This shortfall is called the deflationary gap.
Deflationary Gap = AD at Full Employment − Actual AD

Effects

  • Fall in Aggregate Demand
  • Unsold inventories may increase
  • Firms may reduce production
  • Employment may fall
  • Income may decline
  • There may be downward pressure on prices
Deflationary Gap
9.5

Causes of Excess Demand

Excess demand can arise when different components of Aggregate Demand increase beyond the level required for full-employment equilibrium.

1. Increase in Consumption Expenditure

If households increase consumption expenditure substantially, Aggregate Demand increases. If this increase occurs when the economy is already at full employment, it may generate excess demand.

2. Increase in Investment Expenditure

An increase in investment expenditure directly increases Aggregate Demand. Through the multiplier mechanism, the increase in income and consumption can further strengthen Aggregate Demand.

3. Increase in Government Expenditure

When government expenditure increases, it directly increases Aggregate Demand. If the economy is already operating at full employment, excessive government spending can contribute to excess demand.

4. Reduction in Taxes

A reduction in taxes increases disposable income. Households may spend part of this additional income, increasing consumption and Aggregate Demand.

5. Increase in Exports

Higher exports increase demand for domestically produced goods and services. This raises Aggregate Demand.

6. Reduction in Imports

A fall in imports can increase net exports, other things remaining constant, and therefore increase Aggregate Demand.

7. Increase in Money Supply

An expansion in money supply and availability of credit can encourage consumption and investment expenditure, increasing Aggregate Demand.

AD = C + I + G + (X − M)
9.6

Causes of Deficient Demand

Deficient demand arises when Aggregate Demand is insufficient to support the full-employment level of output.

1. Fall in Consumption Expenditure

If households reduce consumption, firms face lower demand for their goods and services. This reduces Aggregate Demand.

2. Fall in Investment Expenditure

Lower investment reduces Aggregate Demand directly and can also produce a multiplied reduction in income.

3. Fall in Government Expenditure

A reduction in government expenditure directly reduces Aggregate Demand.

4. Increase in Taxes

Higher taxes reduce disposable income. This can reduce consumption expenditure and therefore reduce Aggregate Demand.

5. Fall in Exports

Lower exports reduce foreign demand for domestic goods and services and therefore reduce Aggregate Demand.

6. Increase in Imports

Higher imports, other things remaining constant, reduce net exports and therefore reduce Aggregate Demand.

7. Reduction in Money Supply

A contraction in money supply and credit availability can reduce consumption and investment expenditure, causing Aggregate Demand to decline.

9.7

Measures to Correct Excess Demand – Fiscal Measures

Fiscal policy refers to the policy of the government relating to taxation and government expenditure.

A. Reduction in Government Expenditure

When the government reduces its expenditure on goods and services, Aggregate Demand decreases directly.

If excess demand exists, a reduction in government expenditure can reduce the pressure of demand in the economy.

Government Expenditure ↓ → AD ↓

B. Increase in Taxes

An increase in taxes reduces the disposable income available to households. Lower disposable income tends to reduce consumption expenditure.

Taxes ↑ → Disposable Income ↓ → Consumption ↓ → AD ↓
Objective: Fiscal measures for excess demand are generally contractionary—they aim to reduce Aggregate Demand.
9.8

Measures to Correct Excess Demand – Monetary Measures

Monetary policy refers to the policy through which the monetary authority influences money supply, credit conditions and interest rates in the economy.

A. Reduction in Money Supply

A reduction in money supply can reduce the availability of funds for consumption and investment, thereby reducing Aggregate Demand.

Money Supply ↓ → Spending ↓ → AD ↓

B. Increase in Rate of Interest

A higher rate of interest can discourage borrowing and interest-sensitive investment expenditure. It can therefore help reduce Aggregate Demand.

Interest Rate ↑ → Borrowing / Investment ↓ → AD ↓

C. Restriction of Credit

When credit conditions become tighter, borrowing becomes more difficult or expensive. This can reduce consumption and investment expenditure and therefore reduce Aggregate Demand.

Conclusion:
To correct excess demand, monetary policy generally aims to reduce money and credit availability and/or raise borrowing costs.
9.9

Measures to Correct Deficient Demand – Fiscal Measures

A. Increase in Government Expenditure

An increase in government expenditure directly increases Aggregate Demand.

Government Expenditure ↑ → AD ↑

The initial increase in expenditure may also create additional income, leading to further consumption through the multiplier mechanism.

B. Reduction in Taxes

A reduction in taxes increases disposable income. Households may spend part of this additional income, increasing consumption and Aggregate Demand.

Taxes ↓ → Disposable Income ↑ → Consumption ↑ → AD ↑
Objective: Fiscal measures for deficient demand are generally expansionary—they aim to increase Aggregate Demand.
9.10

Measures to Correct Deficient Demand – Monetary Measures

A. Increase in Money Supply

An increase in money supply can increase the availability of funds and support higher consumption and investment expenditure.

Money Supply ↑ → Spending ↑ → AD ↑

B. Reduction in Rate of Interest

A lower rate of interest can encourage borrowing and investment expenditure.

Interest Rate ↓ → Borrowing / Investment ↑ → AD ↑

C. Expansion of Credit

Easier availability of credit can encourage households and firms to increase expenditure, thereby raising Aggregate Demand.

Conclusion:
To correct deficient demand, monetary policy generally aims to increase money and credit availability and/or reduce borrowing costs.
9.11

Graphical Analysis of Corrective Measures

Correction of Excess Demand

When excess demand exists, the objective of policy is to reduce Aggregate Demand toward the level consistent with full-employment output.

Excess Demand → Contractionary Policy → AD ↓ → Inflationary Pressure ↓

Correction of Deficient Demand

When deficient demand exists, the objective is to increase Aggregate Demand toward the level required to support full-employment output.

Deficient Demand → Expansionary Policy → AD ↑ → Output & Employment ↑

🔴 Excess Demand

Use contractionary fiscal and monetary measures.

G ↓ Taxes ↑ Money Supply ↓ Interest Rate ↑

🟢 Deficient Demand

Use expansionary fiscal and monetary measures.

G ↑ Taxes ↓ Money Supply ↑ Interest Rate ↓

9.13

Solved Practicals

Question 1. At full employment, Aggregate Demand is ₹1,000 crore while actual Aggregate Demand is ₹1,150 crore. Calculate the inflationary gap.
▶ View Answer
Formula:

Inflationary Gap = Actual AD − AD at Full Employment

= ₹1,150 crore − ₹1,000 crore

= ₹150 crore

Therefore, the inflationary gap is ₹150 crore.
Question 2. At full employment, the required Aggregate Demand is ₹2,000 crore, while actual Aggregate Demand is ₹1,700 crore. Find the deflationary gap.
▶ View Answer
Formula:

Deflationary Gap = AD at Full Employment − Actual AD

= ₹2,000 crore − ₹1,700 crore

= ₹300 crore

Therefore, the deflationary gap is ₹300 crore.
Question 3. If government expenditure is increased during deficient demand, what will happen to Aggregate Demand?
▶ View Answer
An increase in government expenditure directly increases Aggregate Demand.

Therefore:

G ↑ → AD ↑

The increase in expenditure can also create additional income and consumption through the multiplier mechanism.
Question 4. What fiscal measures can be used to correct excess demand?
▶ View Answer
The government can use contractionary fiscal measures to reduce excess demand.

1. Reduction in government expenditure: It directly reduces Aggregate Demand.

2. Increase in taxes: It reduces disposable income and consumption expenditure, thereby reducing Aggregate Demand.

Thus, the government can reduce government expenditure and/or increase taxes to control excess demand.
Question 5. What fiscal measures can be used to correct deficient demand?
▶ View Answer
The government can use expansionary fiscal measures.

1. Increase in government expenditure: It directly increases Aggregate Demand.

2. Reduction in taxes: It increases disposable income, which can increase consumption and Aggregate Demand.

Therefore, increased government expenditure and reduced taxes can help correct deficient demand.
Question 6. What happens to Aggregate Demand when the rate of interest is increased to correct excess demand?
▶ View Answer
An increase in the rate of interest tends to make borrowing more expensive. This can discourage investment and some interest-sensitive expenditure.

Therefore:

Interest Rate ↑ → Investment / Borrowing ↓ → AD ↓

Hence, an increase in the rate of interest can help reduce excess demand.
Question 7. How can a reduction in the rate of interest help correct deficient demand?
▶ View Answer
A reduction in the rate of interest can make borrowing less expensive. This may encourage firms to increase investment and can support expenditure.

Therefore:

Interest Rate ↓ → Investment ↑ → AD ↑

Thus, a reduction in the rate of interest can help increase Aggregate Demand during deficient demand.
9.14

Topic-wise Board Questions

Question 1. What is meant by excess demand?
▶ View Answer
Excess demand refers to a situation in which Aggregate Demand is greater than the Aggregate Demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium. It occurs when total planned expenditure exceeds the level necessary to purchase the full-employment level of output. Since the economy is already operating at or near full employment, real output cannot increase proportionately in the short run. Therefore, excess demand creates upward pressure on the general price level.
Question 2. What is meant by deficient demand?
▶ View Answer
Deficient demand refers to a situation in which Aggregate Demand is less than the Aggregate Demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium. When demand is insufficient, firms may be unable to sell the output that could be produced at full employment. They may therefore reduce production and employment. Thus, deficient demand creates a demand deficiency and can lead to deflationary or recessionary pressure.
Question 3. Explain the meaning of inflationary gap.
▶ View Answer
Inflationary gap is the excess of Aggregate Demand over the level of Aggregate Demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium. It occurs when the economy is already at full employment but Aggregate Demand continues to rise. Since productive resources are already highly employed, output cannot increase sufficiently in the short run. The excess demand therefore puts upward pressure on prices.

Inflationary Gap = Actual AD − AD at Full Employment
Question 4. Explain the meaning of deflationary gap.
▶ View Answer
Deflationary gap is the shortfall of Aggregate Demand from the level of Aggregate Demand required to maintain full-employment equilibrium. When Aggregate Demand is insufficient, firms may face difficulty selling their output. They may reduce production and employment, resulting in unemployment and downward pressure on prices.

Deflationary Gap = AD at Full Employment − Actual AD
Question 5. State two fiscal measures to correct excess demand.
▶ View Answer
Two fiscal measures to correct excess demand are:

1. Reduction in government expenditure: It directly reduces Aggregate Demand.

2. Increase in taxes: It reduces disposable income and consumption expenditure, thereby reducing Aggregate Demand.

These are contractionary fiscal measures because their objective is to reduce excessive demand.
Question 6. State two fiscal measures to correct deficient demand.
▶ View Answer
Two fiscal measures to correct deficient demand are:

1. Increase in government expenditure: It directly increases Aggregate Demand.

2. Reduction in taxes: It increases disposable income and can increase consumption expenditure.

These are expansionary fiscal measures because their objective is to increase Aggregate Demand.
Question 7. Explain how monetary policy can be used to correct excess demand.
▶ View Answer
Monetary policy can reduce excess demand through contractionary measures.

The monetary authority can reduce money supply and restrict credit availability. It can also increase the rate of interest.

Higher interest rates can discourage borrowing and investment, while tighter monetary conditions can reduce expenditure.

Therefore:
Money Supply ↓ / Interest Rate ↑ → Expenditure ↓ → AD ↓

This helps reduce excess demand and inflationary pressure.
Question 8. Explain how monetary policy can be used to correct deficient demand.
▶ View Answer
Monetary policy can increase Aggregate Demand through expansionary measures.

The monetary authority can increase money supply, make credit more easily available and reduce the rate of interest.

Lower interest rates can encourage borrowing and investment, increasing expenditure and Aggregate Demand.

Therefore:
Money Supply ↑ / Interest Rate ↓ → Expenditure ↑ → AD ↑

This can help reduce deficient demand.
Question 9. Distinguish between excess demand and deficient demand.
▶ View Answer
Excess demand occurs when Aggregate Demand is greater than the level required at full employment. It creates inflationary pressure.

Deficient demand occurs when Aggregate Demand is less than the level required at full employment. It can lead to lower output, employment and deflationary pressure.

Thus, excess demand requires measures to reduce Aggregate Demand, while deficient demand requires measures to increase Aggregate Demand.
Question 10. Why does excess demand create inflationary pressure?
▶ View Answer
Excess demand occurs when Aggregate Demand exceeds the level required to maintain full-employment output. At full employment, productive resources are already being used to a high degree. Therefore, firms cannot increase real output proportionately in the short run. The additional demand consequently puts upward pressure on the general price level. Hence, excess demand creates inflationary pressure.
Question 11. Why does deficient demand lead to unemployment?
▶ View Answer
Deficient demand means that Aggregate Demand is insufficient to purchase the output corresponding to full employment. When firms cannot sell the desired level of output, they may reduce production. Lower production reduces the demand for labour and other productive resources. Consequently, employment may fall and unemployment may increase.

Case-Based Questions

Case 1: An economy is operating at the full-employment level. Due to a substantial increase in government expenditure and private investment, Aggregate Demand rises considerably beyond the level required to purchase full-employment output.

(a) Identify the situation.
(b) What type of gap is created?
(c) State one fiscal measure to correct it.
(d) State one monetary measure to correct it.
▶ View Answer
(a) The situation is excess demand.

(b) It creates an inflationary gap.

(c) The government can reduce government expenditure or increase taxes.

(d) The monetary authority can reduce money supply or increase the rate of interest.

(a) Identify the situation.
(b) What type of gap is associated with it?
(c) State one fiscal measure.
(d) State one monetary measure.
▶ View Answer
(a) The economy is experiencing deficient demand.

(b) It is associated with a deflationary gap.

(c) The government can increase government expenditure or reduce taxes.

(d) The monetary authority can increase money supply or reduce the rate of interest.

EXAM ALERT

Common Mistakes Students Make

  • Do not define excess demand simply as “demand is greater than supply.” It is defined with reference to the Aggregate Demand required at full employment.
  • Do not confuse excess demand with inflation itself. Excess demand creates inflationary pressure.
  • Do not confuse deficient demand with zero demand. It means demand is insufficient relative to the full-employment requirement.
  • Remember that excess demand is associated with an inflationary gap.
  • Deficient demand is associated with a deflationary gap.
  • For excess demand, use contractionary policies.
  • For deficient demand, use expansionary policies.
  • Excess demand → Government expenditure should generally be reduced, not increased.
  • Deficient demand → Government expenditure should generally be increased, not reduced.
