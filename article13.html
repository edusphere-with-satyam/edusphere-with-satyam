Article 13 of the Constitution of India

Laws inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights

1. Introduction

Article 13 is one of the most important provisions in Part III of the Constitution of India. It deals with laws that are inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights.

Its main purpose is to ensure that ordinary laws cannot take away or abridge the Fundamental Rights guaranteed by the Constitution.

Key Idea: Fundamental Rights are superior to ordinary laws. If a law violates a Fundamental Right, the courts can examine its constitutional validity.

Article 13 is closely connected with the principle of Judicial Review and has played an important role in protecting Fundamental Rights.

2. Constitutional Provision

Article 13 contains four clauses:

  • Article 13(1)
  • Article 13(2)
  • Article 13(3)
  • Article 13(4)

3. Article 13(1) — Pre-Constitution Laws

Article 13(1) deals with laws that were in force before the commencement of the Constitution.

If any provision of such a law is inconsistent with the Fundamental Rights contained in Part III, it becomes void to the extent of the inconsistency.

Simple Meaning

Suppose a law existed before 26 January 1950 and a particular provision of that law violates a Fundamental Right. That conflicting provision cannot continue to operate after the Constitution came into force.

Remember: Article 13(1) mainly deals with pre-Constitution laws.

4. Article 13(2) — Post-Constitution Laws

Article 13(2) provides that the State shall not make any law which takes away or abridges the rights conferred by Part III.

Any law made in contravention of this provision shall be void to the extent of the contravention.

Simple Example

If Parliament or a State Legislature makes an ordinary law that violates a Fundamental Right, a person can challenge that law before the appropriate court.

Remember: Article 13(2) mainly deals with post-Constitution laws.

5. Article 13(3) — Meaning of "Law"

Article 13(3) explains what is included within the expression "law" for the purposes of Article 13.

Article 13(3)(a)

The term "law" includes:

  • Ordinance
  • Order
  • Bye-law
  • Rule
  • Regulation
  • Notification
  • Custom
  • Usage

These are included when they have the force of law in the territory of India.

Article 13(3)(b)

"Laws in force" refers to laws made or passed by a Legislature or other competent authority before the commencement of the Constitution and not previously repealed.

6. Article 13(4) — Constitutional Amendments

Article 13(4) was inserted by the 24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971.

It provides that Article 13 shall not apply to any amendment of the Constitution made under Article 368.

Why was Article 13(4) inserted?

A major constitutional controversy arose regarding whether Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights using its power under Article 368.

The 24th Amendment attempted to clarify Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

However, the Supreme Court later developed the Basic Structure Doctrine, which places limits on Parliament's amending power.

7. Article 13 and Judicial Review

Judicial Review means the power of courts to examine laws and governmental actions and determine whether they are consistent with the Constitution.

If a law violates the Constitution, the court may declare the offending provision unconstitutional to the extent of the violation.

Article 13 provides important constitutional support for the protection of Fundamental Rights through judicial review.

8. Important Doctrines

A. Doctrine of Severability

The Doctrine of Severability means that if only a particular part of a law violates a Fundamental Right, the unconstitutional part can be separated from the valid part.

If the valid portion can function independently, it may continue to operate.

Landmark Case: R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India (1957)

B. Doctrine of Eclipse

The Doctrine of Eclipse is mainly associated with pre-Constitution laws that are inconsistent with Fundamental Rights.

Such a law becomes inoperative to the extent of its inconsistency with Fundamental Rights.

Landmark Case: Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955)

C. Doctrine of Waiver

Waiver generally means voluntarily giving up a known right. However, the Supreme Court has held that Fundamental Rights cannot generally be waived by an individual.

Landmark Case: Basheshar Nath v. Commissioner of Income Tax (1959)

9. Important Landmark Cases

Keshavan Madhava Menon v. State of Bombay (1951)

The case dealt with the effect of Article 13(1) on pre-Constitution laws and became important in understanding their operation after the Constitution came into force.

Shankari Prasad Singh Deo v. Union of India (1951)

The Supreme Court held that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not "law" within the meaning of Article 13.

Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

The Supreme Court again upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights under Article 368.

I.C. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights in a way that abridged or took them away.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament has wide power to amend the Constitution, but it cannot destroy or alter its Basic Structure.

Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India (1980)

The Supreme Court reaffirmed that Parliament's amending power is limited and cannot become an unlimited power.

11. Important Constitutional Amendments

24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

The 24th Amendment inserted Article 13(4) and clarified that Article 13 would not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368.

42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976

The 42nd Amendment attempted to give very wide protection to constitutional amendments from judicial review.

Important aspects of these changes were subsequently invalidated by the Supreme Court, including in the Minerva Mills case.

12. Article 13 vs Article 368

Article 13 Article 368
Deals with laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights. Deals with the constitutional amendment process.
Protects Fundamental Rights from inconsistent laws. Provides the constitutional procedure and power for amendment.
Article 13(2) restricts the State from making laws that abridge Fundamental Rights. Parliament can amend the Constitution subject to constitutional limitations.
Article 13(4) excludes constitutional amendments under Article 368 from Article 13. Amending power is subject to the Basic Structure Doctrine.
