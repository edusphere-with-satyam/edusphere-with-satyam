Government Budget is an important instrument of fiscal policy. It shows the estimated receipts and expenditure of the government for a financial year and helps the government achieve economic and social objectives.

1. GOVERNMENT BUDGET

🏛️ Meaning of Government Budget

Definition:
A Government Budget is an annual statement showing the estimated receipts and estimated expenditure of the government during a financial year.
Simple Meaning:
Just as a family prepares a plan for its expected income and expenditure, the government also prepares a financial plan for a financial year.
Exam Point:
Government Budget is an annual statement of estimated receipts and expenditure.
2. OBJECTIVES

🎯 Objectives of Government Budget

1. Allocation of Resources

The government allocates resources towards socially desirable activities such as education, health, infrastructure and defence.

Example:
Government expenditure on public hospitals and schools helps allocate resources towards important social services.

2. Redistribution of Income and Wealth

The government attempts to reduce inequalities of income and wealth through taxation, subsidies and welfare programmes.

3. Economic Stability

The government uses taxation and expenditure policies to control inflation, unemployment and fluctuations in economic activity.

4. Economic Growth

Government expenditure on infrastructure, education, health and productive activities promotes economic growth.

5. Provision of Public Goods

The government provides goods and services such as defence, roads, public infrastructure and administration.

3. COMPONENTS

📊 Components of Government Budget

Government Budget has two major components:

1. Budget Receipts
2. Budget Expenditure
Component Meaning
Budget Receipts Estimated money received by the government
Budget Expenditure Estimated expenditure of the government
4. BUDGET RECEIPTS

💰 Budget Receipts

Budget receipts refer to the estimated money received by the government during a financial year.

Budget Receipts are broadly divided into:

1. Revenue Receipts
2. Capital Receipts
5. REVENUE RECEIPTS

💵 Revenue Receipts

Definition:
Revenue receipts are those receipts which neither create a liability for the government nor cause a reduction in government assets.
Two Types:

1. Tax Revenue
2. Non-Tax Revenue

Tax Revenue

Tax revenue is the revenue received by the government through taxes imposed on individuals, firms and other economic activities.

Examples:
Income Tax, Corporation Tax, GST and Customs Duty.

Non-Tax Revenue

Non-tax revenue is the revenue received by the government from sources other than taxes.

Examples:
Fees, fines, penalties, interest receipts, dividends and profits of public sector enterprises.
Revenue Receipts = Tax Revenue + Non-Tax Revenue
6. CAPITAL RECEIPTS

🏦 Capital Receipts

Definition:
Capital receipts are those receipts which either create a liability for the government or cause a reduction in government assets.
Major Sources of Capital Receipts:

• Borrowings
• Recovery of Loans
• Disinvestment

1. Borrowings

Borrowings create a liability for the government because the borrowed amount has to be repaid in the future.

2. Recovery of Loans

When the government recovers loans previously given by it, its financial assets are reduced.

3. Disinvestment

Disinvestment means selling a part or whole of the government's shareholding in a public sector enterprise.

Remember:
Capital Receipt → Creates Liability OR Reduces Asset
7. BUDGET EXPENDITURE

📤 Budget Expenditure

Definition:
Budget expenditure refers to the estimated expenditure of the government during a financial year.
Two Major Types:

1. Revenue Expenditure
2. Capital Expenditure
8. REVENUE EXPENDITURE

💸 Revenue Expenditure

Definition:
Revenue expenditure is expenditure that does not result in the creation of physical or financial assets and does not directly reduce a liability.
Examples:

• Salaries of government employees
• Pensions
• Interest payments
• Subsidies
• Routine administrative expenditure
Remember:
Revenue Expenditure → Generally does not create assets.
9. CAPITAL EXPENDITURE

🏗️ Capital Expenditure

Definition:
Capital expenditure is expenditure that leads to the creation of physical or financial assets or reduction of liabilities.
Examples:

• Construction of roads and bridges
• Construction of schools and hospitals
• Purchase of machinery
• Repayment of government debt
Remember:
Capital Expenditure → Creates Assets OR Reduces Liabilities
10. COMPARISON

⚖️ Revenue Expenditure vs Capital Expenditure

Basis Revenue Expenditure Capital Expenditure
Asset Creation Generally does not create assets Creates assets
Liability Generally does not reduce liabilities May reduce liabilities
Examples Salaries, pensions, subsidies Roads, machinery, debt repayment
11. TYPES OF BUDGET

⚖️ Balanced, Surplus and Deficit Budget

1. Balanced Budget

A budget is balanced when estimated government receipts are equal to estimated government expenditure.
Budget Receipts = Budget Expenditure

2. Surplus Budget

A budget is a surplus budget when estimated government receipts are greater than estimated government expenditure.
Budget Receipts > Budget Expenditure

3. Deficit Budget

A budget is a deficit budget when estimated government expenditure is greater than estimated government receipts.
Budget Expenditure > Budget Receipts
12. REVENUE DEFICIT

🔴 Revenue Deficit

Definition:
Revenue deficit refers to the excess of revenue expenditure over revenue receipts.
Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts
Exam Point:
Revenue deficit indicates that revenue expenditure is greater than revenue receipts.
13. FISCAL DEFICIT

🔴 Fiscal Deficit

Definition:
Fiscal deficit is the excess of total expenditure over total receipts excluding borrowings.
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding Borrowings
Important:
Fiscal deficit indicates the borrowing requirement of the government.
14. PRIMARY DEFICIT

🟠 Primary Deficit

Definition:
Primary deficit is fiscal deficit minus interest payments.
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments
Example:

Fiscal Deficit = ₹500 crore
Interest Payments = ₹150 crore

Primary Deficit = ₹500 − ₹150

Primary Deficit = ₹350 crore
15. FISCAL POLICY

📈 Fiscal Policy

Definition:
Fiscal policy refers to the policy of the government regarding taxation, public expenditure and borrowing to influence economic activity.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy

Expansionary fiscal policy aims to increase aggregate demand and economic activity.

Measures:

• Increase in government expenditure
• Reduction in taxes

Contractionary Fiscal Policy

Contractionary fiscal policy aims to reduce excessive aggregate demand and inflationary pressure.

Measures:

• Reduction in government expenditure
• Increase in taxes
16. ECONOMIC STABILITY

📊 Government Budget and Economic Stability

During Inflation:

The government may reduce expenditure or increase taxes to reduce excessive aggregate demand.
During Recession:

The government may increase expenditure or reduce taxes to stimulate aggregate demand and economic activity.
17. EXTRA KNOWLEDGE

🇮🇳 How Does the Union Budget Pass?

Step 1 — Preparation:
The Ministry of Finance prepares the Union Budget after receiving estimates from different ministries and departments.
Step 2 — Presentation:
The Union Budget is presented in Parliament by the Union Finance Minister.
Step 3 — General Discussion:
Members of Parliament discuss the overall budget proposals and government financial policy.
Step 4 — Demands for Grants:
The Lok Sabha discusses and votes on the demands for grants of different ministries.
Step 5 — Appropriation Bill:
The Appropriation Bill authorises withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of India for government expenditure.
Step 6 — Finance Bill:
The Finance Bill contains proposals relating to taxation and other financial matters.
Step 7 — President's Assent:
After being passed by Parliament, the relevant bill is sent to the President for assent.

19. HISTORY

📜 India's First Budget

First Budget of Independent India:

The first Budget of independent India was presented on 26 November 1947 by R. K. Shanmukham Chetty, the first Finance Minister of independent India.
Interesting Fact:

The first Budget after independence covered the period from 15 August 1947 to 31 March 1948.
