Government Budget — Definition:

A Government Budget is an annual statement showing the estimated receipts and estimated expenditure of the government during a financial year.
Simple Example:

Just as a family plans its expected income and expenditure, the government prepares a financial plan for the coming financial year.
1. Objectives

🎯 Objectives of Government Budget

1. Allocation of Resources

The government uses the budget to allocate resources towards socially important activities such as education, health, infrastructure and defence.
2. Redistribution of Income and Wealth

The government can reduce economic inequalities through progressive taxation, subsidies and welfare expenditure.
3. Economic Stability

The government uses taxation and expenditure policies to control inflation, unemployment and fluctuations in economic activity.
4. Economic Growth

Government expenditure on infrastructure, education, health and other productive activities can promote economic growth.
5. Management of Public Enterprises

The government provides financial support and makes plans for public sector enterprises and public services.
2. Components

📊 Components of Government Budget

Government Budget has two major components:

1. Budget Receipts
2. Budget Expenditure
Budget Receipts:

Budget receipts refer to the estimated money received by the government during a financial year.
Budget Expenditure:

Budget expenditure refers to the estimated expenditure of the government during a financial year.
3. Revenue Receipts

💰 Revenue Receipts

Definition:

Revenue receipts are those receipts which neither create a liability for the government nor cause a reduction in government assets.

Major Types of Revenue Receipts

Tax Revenue

Tax revenue is the revenue received by the government through taxes imposed on individuals, businesses and other economic activities.

Examples:

Income Tax, Corporation Tax, Goods and Services Tax (GST), Customs Duty and Excise Duty.

Non-Tax Revenue

Non-tax revenue is the revenue received by the government from sources other than taxes.

Examples:

Fees, fines, penalties, interest receipts, dividends and profits from public sector enterprises.
Remember:

Revenue Receipts = Tax Revenue + Non-Tax Revenue
4. Capital Receipts

🏦 Capital Receipts

Definition:

Capital receipts are those receipts which either create a liability for the government or cause a reduction in government assets.

Major Sources

1. Borrowings

Borrowings create a liability for the government because the borrowed amount has to be repaid in the future.
2. Recovery of Loans

When the government recovers loans previously given to states, public enterprises or other entities, it receives money and reduces its financial assets.
3. Disinvestment

Disinvestment means selling a part or whole of the government's shareholding in a public sector enterprise.
Easy Rule:

Capital Receipt → Creates Liability OR Reduces Asset
5. Budget Expenditure

📤 Budget Expenditure

Definition:

Budget expenditure refers to the estimated expenditure of the government during a financial year.
Budget expenditure is broadly classified into:

1. Revenue Expenditure
2. Capital Expenditure
6. Revenue Expenditure

💸 Revenue Expenditure

Definition:

Revenue expenditure is expenditure that does not result in the creation of physical or financial assets and does not directly reduce a liability.
Examples:

Salaries of government employees, pensions, interest payments, subsidies and expenditure on routine administration.
Remember:

Revenue Expenditure → Generally does not create assets.
7. Capital Expenditure

🏗️ Capital Expenditure

Definition:

Capital expenditure is expenditure that leads to the creation of physical or financial assets or reduction of liabilities.
Examples:

Construction of roads, bridges, schools and hospitals, purchase of machinery and repayment of government debt.
Remember:

Capital Expenditure → Creates Assets OR Reduces Liabilities
8. Difference

📋 Revenue vs Capital

Basis Revenue Capital
Effect on assets Generally does not create assets Creates assets or reduces liabilities
Effect on liabilities Generally does not reduce liabilities May reduce liabilities
Examples Salaries, pensions, subsidies Roads, machinery, repayment of debt
9. Types of Budget

⚖️ Balanced, Surplus and Deficit Budget

Balanced Budget:

A budget is balanced when estimated government receipts are equal to estimated government expenditure.
Budget Receipts = Budget Expenditure
Surplus Budget:

A budget is a surplus budget when estimated government receipts are greater than estimated government expenditure.
Budget Receipts > Budget Expenditure
Deficit Budget:

A budget is a deficit budget when estimated government expenditure is greater than estimated government receipts.
Budget Expenditure > Budget Receipts
10. Revenue Deficit

🔴 Revenue Deficit

Definition:

Revenue deficit refers to the excess of revenue expenditure over revenue receipts.
Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts
Meaning:

A revenue deficit indicates that the government is not able to meet its revenue expenditure from its revenue receipts.
11. Fiscal Deficit

🔴 Fiscal Deficit

Definition:

Fiscal deficit is the excess of total expenditure over total receipts excluding borrowings.
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding Borrowings
Important:

Fiscal deficit indicates the borrowing requirement of the government.
12. Primary Deficit

🟠 Primary Deficit

Definition:

Primary deficit is fiscal deficit minus interest payments.
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments
Example:

If Fiscal Deficit = ₹500 crore and Interest Payments = ₹150 crore:

Primary Deficit = ₹500 − ₹150

Primary Deficit = ₹350 crore
13. Deficit Relationships

🔗 Important Deficit Formulas

Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding Borrowings
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments
Board Exam Tip:

Always write the formula first and then substitute the values when solving numerical questions.
14. Fiscal Policy

📈 Fiscal Policy

Definition:

Fiscal policy refers to the policy of the government regarding taxation, public expenditure and borrowing to influence economic activity.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy

The government may increase expenditure or reduce taxes to increase aggregate demand and stimulate economic activity.

Contractionary Fiscal Policy

The government may reduce expenditure or increase taxes to control excessive aggregate demand and inflationary pressure.
15. Economic Stability

📊 Government Budget and Economic Stability

During Inflation:

The government may reduce expenditure or increase taxes to reduce excessive aggregate demand.
During Recession:

The government may increase expenditure or reduce taxes to stimulate aggregate demand and economic activity.
⭐ Extra Knowledge

🇮🇳 How Does the Government Budget Get Passed?

Do you know?

The Union Budget is not simply announced and immediately implemented. It goes through a constitutional and parliamentary process.
Step 1 — Preparation of Budget

The Ministry of Finance prepares the Union Budget after collecting estimates of government receipts and expenditure from different ministries and departments.
Step 2 — Presentation in Parliament

The Union Budget is presented in Parliament by the Union Finance Minister.
Step 3 — General Discussion

Members of Parliament discuss the overall budget proposals and government's financial policy.
Step 4 — Examination by Parliamentary Committees

The demands for grants of different ministries are examined by the relevant Parliamentary Standing Committees.
Step 5 — Demands for Grants

The Lok Sabha discusses and votes on the demands for grants of different ministries.
Step 6 — Appropriation Bill

After the demands for grants are voted, an Appropriation Bill is introduced to authorise withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of India.
Step 7 — Finance Bill

The Finance Bill contains proposals relating to taxation and other financial matters.
Step 8 — President's Assent

After being passed by Parliament, the relevant bills are sent to the President for assent according to the constitutional process.

📜 History

🇮🇳 India's First Budget

First Budget of Independent India:

The first Budget of independent India was presented on 26 November 1947 by R. K. Shanmukham Chetty, who was the first Finance Minister of independent India.
Interesting Fact:

The first Budget after independence covered the period from 15 August 1947 to 31 March 1948.
