📚 Introduction to Market and Perfect Competition

A market is an arrangement through which buyers and sellers interact with each other for the purchase and sale of goods and services. A market does not necessarily mean a physical place. Buyers and sellers can interact through shops, markets, telephone, websites, applications or other communication systems.

Markets differ according to the number of buyers and sellers, nature of the product, degree of competition and the ease with which firms can enter or leave the market.

The major forms of market structure studied in economics include perfect competition, monopoly, monopolistic competition and oligopoly.

Simple idea: Market structure tells us how a market is organised and how much control buyers and sellers have over price and output.

🏪 Meaning of Perfect Competition

Formal Definition: Perfect competition is a market structure in which there are a very large number of buyers and sellers, firms sell a homogeneous product, and no individual firm is able to influence the market price.
Simple Definition: Perfect competition is a market where there are many buyers and sellers, the product is identical, and an individual firm has no power to decide the market price.

The word perfect does not mean that every real-world market actually satisfies all these conditions. Perfect competition is primarily an economic model used to understand how price and output are determined when competition is extremely strong.

🔑 Features of Perfect Competition

Large Number of Buyers and Sellers

There are a very large number of buyers and sellers. Each individual buyer purchases only a small part of total market output, while each individual firm produces only a small proportion of total market supply.

Because an individual firm represents only a very small part of the market, changing its own output has no significant effect on the total market supply. Therefore, it cannot independently determine the market price.

Homogeneous Product

All firms sell a homogeneous product. This means that the products supplied by different firms are identical in terms of their essential characteristics. Buyers therefore do not have a reason to prefer one seller's product over another merely because of product differences.

If products are identical and available at the same market price, a buyer would generally be willing to purchase from any seller.

Free Entry and Exit of Firms

There are no significant restrictions preventing firms from entering the industry when profitable opportunities exist or leaving the industry when they cannot earn sufficient returns.

This assumption is important because the entry and exit of firms can influence market supply and therefore affect the market price over time.

Perfect Knowledge

Buyers and sellers are assumed to have complete knowledge about relevant market conditions, particularly the prevailing price.

If the same homogeneous product were sold at different prices and buyers knew about the difference, they would prefer the lower-priced seller. Perfect knowledge therefore supports the existence of a common market price.

Perfect Mobility of Factors of Production

Factors of production are assumed to be freely mobile between different uses and firms. Resources can move toward activities where they are more productively or profitably employed.

Price-Taking Behaviour

An individual firm is a price taker. It accepts the price determined by market demand and market supply.

Important: A price-taking firm does not mean that the firm receives no revenue. It means that the individual firm cannot independently change the market price by changing its own output.

Low or No Significant Selling Costs

Since firms sell homogeneous products and buyers are assumed to have perfect knowledge, there is little need for advertising or other methods designed to differentiate one firm's product from another.

🏭 Firm and Industry

Firm: A firm is an individual production unit that produces and sells a commodity.
Industry: An industry is the collection of all firms producing the same or closely related commodity.
Example: If there are 1,000 firms producing the same commodity, each individual producer is a firm, while all 1,000 firms together constitute the industry.
Firm Industry
Individual producer Collection of all firms producing the commodity
Small share of total market supply Determines total market supply
Takes the market price Market demand and supply determine equilibrium price

📉 Market Demand

Formal Definition: Market demand refers to the total quantity of a commodity demanded by all buyers in the market at different possible prices during a given period.

Market demand is obtained by adding the quantities demanded by all individual buyers at each price.

Market Demand = Sum of Individual Demands

The market demand curve generally slopes downward from left to right. This indicates an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded, other things remaining constant.

📈 Market Supply

Formal Definition: Market supply refers to the total quantity of a commodity that all firms are willing and able to supply at different possible prices during a given period.

Market supply is obtained by adding the quantities supplied by all individual firms at each price.

Market Supply = Sum of Individual Supplies

The market supply curve generally slopes upward from left to right, indicating that producers are generally willing to supply more at higher prices, other things remaining constant.

⚖️ Interaction of Market Demand and Market Supply

📊 View Demand–Supply Graph and Interpretation

Detailed Graph Interpretation

The downward-sloping D curve represents market demand. The upward-sloping S curve represents market supply.

The two curves intersect at point E. This point represents market equilibrium because at this price and quantity, the quantity demanded is equal to the quantity supplied.

The corresponding price is called the equilibrium price (Pe) and the corresponding quantity is called the equilibrium quantity (Qe).

At equilibrium: Quantity Demanded = Quantity Supplied

There is no pressure for the market price to move away from equilibrium when the quantities demanded and supplied are equal, assuming other conditions remain unchanged.

⚖️ Market Equilibrium

Formal Definition: Market equilibrium is a situation in which the quantity demanded of a commodity is equal to its quantity supplied at a particular price.
Simple Definition: Market equilibrium is the point where buyers want to buy exactly the quantity that sellers want to sell at the prevailing price.

Equilibrium Price

The price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied is known as the equilibrium price.

Equilibrium Quantity

The quantity bought and sold at the equilibrium price is called the equilibrium quantity.

Qd = Qs

🔴 Excess Demand

Definition: Excess demand occurs when quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied at a particular price.
Excess Demand = Qd − Qs

Suppose at a price of ₹20, buyers want to purchase 80 units while sellers are willing to supply only 60 units.

Excess Demand = 80 − 60 = 20 units

There is a shortage of 20 units. Buyers compete for the limited quantity available, creating upward pressure on price.

Below equilibrium price → generally excess demand → upward pressure on price.

🔵 Excess Supply

Definition: Excess supply occurs when quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded at a particular price.
Excess Supply = Qs − Qd

Suppose at a price of ₹40, sellers want to supply 80 units while buyers want to purchase only 40 units.

Excess Supply = 80 − 40 = 40 units

There is a surplus of 40 units. Sellers may reduce prices to attract buyers, creating downward pressure on price.

Above equilibrium price → generally excess supply → downward pressure on price.

🔄 Adjustment toward Equilibrium

The market tends toward equilibrium through changes in price when there is excess demand or excess supply.

Situation Condition Pressure on Price
Excess Demand Qd > Qs Upward
Equilibrium Qd = Qs No pressure for change
Excess Supply Qs > Qd Downward

This adjustment process helps explain why the intersection of demand and supply is important in determining the equilibrium price.

📈 Change in Demand and New Equilibrium

A change in demand means a shift of the entire demand curve. It occurs because of factors other than the commodity's own price, such as income, tastes, preferences, prices of related goods, population or expectations.

Increase in Demand

An increase in demand shifts the demand curve to the right, assuming supply remains unchanged. The new intersection generally results in a higher equilibrium price and higher equilibrium quantity.

📊 View Increase in Demand Graph and Interpretation

Detailed Interpretation

The original equilibrium is determined by the intersection of D₁ and S. When demand increases, the demand curve shifts rightward from D₁ to D₂.

At the original price, buyers now want to purchase more than firms are willing to supply. This creates excess demand.

The resulting pressure on price raises the equilibrium price. As firms respond to the higher price by supplying more, the new equilibrium quantity also increases.

Result: With supply unchanged, an increase in demand generally raises both equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.

Decrease in Demand

A decrease in demand shifts the demand curve to the left. With supply unchanged, the new equilibrium generally involves a lower equilibrium price and lower equilibrium quantity.

📉 Change in Supply and New Equilibrium

A change in supply means a shift of the entire supply curve. It can result from changes in input prices, technology, taxes, subsidies, number of firms, expectations and other supply-side factors.

Increase in Supply

An increase in supply shifts the supply curve to the right. With demand unchanged, the new equilibrium generally results in a lower equilibrium price and higher equilibrium quantity.

📊 View Increase in Supply Graph and Interpretation

Detailed Interpretation

The original equilibrium is determined by D and S₁. When supply increases, the supply curve shifts rightward from S₁ to S₂.

At the original price, firms are now willing to supply more than buyers want to purchase. This creates downward pressure on price.

The lower price encourages greater quantity demanded, resulting in a new equilibrium with a higher quantity and a lower price.

Result: With demand unchanged, an increase in supply generally reduces equilibrium price and increases equilibrium quantity.

Decrease in Supply

A decrease in supply shifts the supply curve to the left. With demand unchanged, the equilibrium price generally rises while equilibrium quantity falls.

↔️ Movement along a Curve vs Shift of a Curve

This distinction is extremely important in demand and supply analysis.

Movement along Curve Shift of Curve
Caused by change in the commodity's own price. Caused by factors other than the commodity's own price.
Occurs on the same curve. The entire curve moves to a new position.
For example, expansion or contraction of demand. For example, change in income or population causing demand to shift.
For supply, a change in own price causes movement along the same supply curve. Technology, input prices, taxes or number of firms can shift supply.
Remember: Change in own price → movement along the curve. Other determinants → shift of the curve.

🛒 Determinants of Demand

Demand for a commodity is influenced by several factors other than its own price. A change in these factors can shift the demand curve.

Factor Possible Effect
Income of consumers Can increase or decrease demand depending on the nature of the good.
Prices of related goods Changes in prices of substitutes and complementary goods can affect demand.
Tastes and preferences Favourable preferences can increase demand.
Population An increase in the number of consumers can increase market demand.
Future expectations Expected future price or income changes can influence present demand.

🏭 Determinants of Supply

Supply is influenced by several factors other than the commodity's own price. Changes in these factors shift the supply curve.

Factor Effect on Supply
Input prices Lower input costs can increase supply.
Technology Improved technology can increase productive capacity and supply.
Taxes Higher production taxes can reduce supply, other things remaining constant.
Subsidies Subsidies can encourage production and increase supply.
Number of firms More firms generally increase market supply.
Future expectations Expected future conditions can influence present supply.

🔄 Simultaneous Changes in Demand and Supply

Sometimes demand and supply change at the same time. In such situations, the effect on equilibrium price and quantity depends on the direction and magnitude of the two shifts.

Change Likely Effect on Price Likely Effect on Quantity
Demand increases Rises Rises
Demand decreases Falls Falls
Supply increases Falls Rises
Supply decreases Rises Falls
When demand and supply change simultaneously, do not memorise a single result for price. Analyse the relative size and direction of the shifts.

🧮 Five Strong Numerical Questions

Question 1:
At a price of ₹20, quantity demanded is 80 units and quantity supplied is 50 units. Calculate excess demand.
View Answer
Excess Demand = Qd − Qs = 80 − 50 = 30 units Therefore, the market has an excess demand of 30 units.
Question 2:
At different prices, the following quantities are demanded and supplied:
Price Qd Qs
₹10 100 40
₹20 80 60
₹30 60 60
₹40 40 80
Find equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.
View Answer
At equilibrium: Qd = Qs At ₹30: Qd = 60 units Qs = 60 units Therefore: Equilibrium Price = ₹30 Equilibrium Quantity = 60 units
Question 3:
Market demand is given by Qd = 100 − 2P and market supply is given by Qs = 20 + 2P. Find the equilibrium price and quantity.
View Answer
At equilibrium: Qd = Qs Therefore: 100 − 2P = 20 + 2P 100 − 20 = 2P + 2P 80 = 4P P = ₹20 Substitute P = 20 into the demand equation: Qd = 100 − 2(20) Qd = 100 − 40 Qd = 60 units Therefore: Equilibrium Price = ₹20 Equilibrium Quantity = 60 units
Question 4:
Market demand is Qd = 120 − 2P and market supply is Qs = 20 + 3P. Find the equilibrium price and quantity.
View Answer
At equilibrium: 120 − 2P = 20 + 3P 120 − 20 = 3P + 2P 100 = 5P P = ₹20 Now substitute P = 20: Qd = 120 − 2(20) = 120 − 40 = 80 units Therefore: Equilibrium Price = ₹20 Equilibrium Quantity = 80 units
Question 5:
The equilibrium price of a commodity is ₹50 and the equilibrium quantity is 100 units. Due to an increase in demand, at the original price buyers now want to purchase 130 units while sellers continue to supply 100 units. Explain the market situation and the direction in which equilibrium price is expected to move, assuming supply remains unchanged.
View Answer
At the original price: Quantity Demanded = 130 units Quantity Supplied = 100 units Therefore: Excess Demand = 130 − 100 = 30 units The market has excess demand. Buyers compete for the limited available supply, creating upward pressure on price. As price rises, producers are willing to supply more and consumers generally demand less. The market moves toward a new equilibrium. Therefore: The equilibrium price is expected to rise. With supply unchanged, an increase in demand generally also results in a higher equilibrium quantity.

⚠️ Important Exam Points and Common Confusions

1. Price Taker does not mean Price Maker

A perfectly competitive firm accepts the market price. It cannot independently fix a different market price because its share of total market supply is insignificant.

2. Market Demand is not Individual Demand

Individual demand belongs to one consumer. Market demand is the sum of the demands of all consumers.

3. Market Supply is not Individual Supply

Individual supply belongs to one firm. Market supply is the total supply of all firms.

4. Equilibrium does not mean Maximum Price

Equilibrium is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. It is not necessarily the highest or lowest possible price.

5. Movement is not Shift

A change in the commodity's own price causes movement along the demand or supply curve. Changes in other determinants cause the entire curve to shift.

6. Excess Demand and Excess Supply

Below equilibrium price, there is generally excess demand. Above equilibrium price, there is generally excess supply, assuming the usual downward-sloping demand and upward-sloping supply curves.
