📘 Unit 1 — National Income and Related Aggregates

What you will learn:

National Income, Domestic Product, National Product, GDP, GNP, NDP, NNP, Market Price, Factor Cost, depreciation, NFIA, final and intermediate goods, double counting, value added, income method, expenditure method and important numericals.

National income accounting is a systematic method of measuring the economic activities and performance of an economy during a given accounting period.

1. Basic Concepts

📚 Basic Concepts of National Income

National Income

National income is the sum of factor incomes earned by the normal residents of a country during an accounting year.
Domestic Product

Domestic product refers to the value of final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during an accounting year.
National Product

National product refers to the value of final goods and services produced by the normal residents of a country during an accounting year.
2. Domestic Territory

🌍 Domestic Territory

Domestic territory refers to the economic territory under the administrative control of a country within which economic activities take place.
Important:
Domestic territory is different from geographical territory. Certain economic territories outside the geographical boundaries may also be included according to national accounting rules.
3. Normal Resident

👤 Normal Resident

A normal resident is a person or institution whose centre of economic interest lies in the economic territory of a country and who normally resides there for a sufficiently long period.
Example:
An Indian resident working temporarily abroad may continue to be treated as a normal resident of India, depending on the circumstances.
4. Final and Intermediate Goods

🛒 Final Goods and Intermediate Goods

Final Goods

Final goods are goods purchased for final consumption or investment and are not meant for resale or further processing during the accounting period.
Intermediate Goods

Intermediate goods are goods used as inputs in the production of other goods and services during the same accounting period.
Basis Final Goods Intermediate Goods
Purpose Final use Further production
Use Consumption or investment Used as input
National income Included Not counted separately
Example Car purchased by a household Steel used by a car manufacturer
Why are intermediate goods not counted separately?

Because doing so may result in double counting.
5. Stock and Flow

📊 Stock and Flow

Stock

A stock variable is measured at a particular point of time.

Examples: Wealth, capital stock and money balance.
Flow

A flow variable is measured over a period of time.

Examples: National income, production, consumption and investment.
Easy Trick:

Stock → Point of time
Flow → Period of time
6. Depreciation

⚙️ Depreciation

Depreciation refers to the fall in the value of fixed assets due to normal wear and tear, passage of time and expected obsolescence.
Net Investment = Gross Investment − Depreciation
Remember:
Gross → subtract depreciation → Net
7. NFIA

🌐 Net Factor Income from Abroad

NFIA is the difference between factor income received from abroad and factor income paid to the rest of the world.
NFIA = Factor Income from Abroad − Factor Income to Abroad
National Product = Domestic Product + NFIA
8. National Income Aggregates

⭐ 8 Important Aggregates of National Income

The following eight aggregates are extremely important for board examinations and numerical questions.

1. GDP at Market Price (GDPMP)

GDP at Market Price is the market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during an accounting year.
GDPMP = Value of final goods and services produced within domestic territory at market prices

2. GDP at Factor Cost (GDPFC)

GDP at Factor Cost measures the value of domestic product in terms of factor payments.
GDPFC = GDPMP − Net Indirect Taxes

3. NDP at Market Price (NDPMP)

NDP at Market Price is GDP at Market Price after deducting depreciation.
NDPMP = GDPMP − Depreciation

4. NDP at Factor Cost (NDPFC)

NDP at Factor Cost is the net domestic product valued at factor cost.
NDPFC = NDPMP − Net Indirect Taxes

5. GNP at Market Price (GNPMP)

GNP at Market Price is GDP at Market Price adjusted for Net Factor Income from Abroad.
GNPMP = GDPMP + NFIA

6. GNP at Factor Cost (GNPFC)

GNP at Factor Cost is GNP at Market Price after deducting Net Indirect Taxes.
GNPFC = GNPMP − Net Indirect Taxes

7. NNP at Market Price (NNPMP)

NNP at Market Price is GNP at Market Price after deducting depreciation.
NNPMP = GNPMP − Depreciation

8. NNP at Factor Cost (NNPFC)

NNP at Factor Cost is the net national product valued at factor cost. It is commonly known as National Income.
National Income = NNPFC

🔄 National Income Conversion Rules

Conversion What to Do
Gross → Net − Depreciation
Net → Gross + Depreciation
Domestic → National + NFIA
National → Domestic − NFIA
Market Price → Factor Cost − Net Indirect Taxes
Factor Cost → Market Price + Net Indirect Taxes
Master Trick:

Gross → Net = Minus Depreciation
Domestic → National = Plus NFIA
MP → FC = Minus Net Indirect Taxes
9. Double Counting

⚠️ Problem of Double Counting

Double counting means counting the value of the same product or service more than once while calculating national income.
Example:

Suppose wheat is sold to a flour mill and the flour is then sold to a bakery. If the value of wheat and flour is counted separately along with the value of the final product, the same economic activity may be counted more than once.
Methods to Avoid Double Counting:
  • Count only the value of final goods and services.
  • Use the Value Added Method.
10A. Value Added Method

🏭 Value Added Method

Under the Value Added Method, national income is estimated by adding the value added by all producing units or sectors of the economy during an accounting year.

What is Value Added?

Value Added is the addition to the value of goods and services at each stage of production.
Value Added = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption

Components of Value Added

Component Meaning
Value of Output Total value of goods and services produced
Intermediate Consumption Value of goods and services used as inputs during production
Gross Value Added Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption
Depreciation Loss in value of fixed assets due to normal wear and tear
Net Value Added Gross Value Added − Depreciation
Net Indirect Taxes Indirect taxes − subsidies

Step-by-Step Understanding

Step 1: Find the value of output.

Step 2: Subtract intermediate consumption.

Step 3: The result is Gross Value Added.

Step 4: Subtract depreciation to obtain Net Value Added.

Step 5: If required, adjust for Net Indirect Taxes and NFIA to reach the required national income aggregate.
GVA = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption
NVA = GVA − Depreciation
Example:

Value of Output = ₹1,000 crore
Intermediate Consumption = ₹400 crore

GVA = ₹1,000 − ₹400

GVA = ₹600 crore
Board Exam Point:

The Value Added Method avoids double counting because only the additional value created at each stage of production is counted.
10B. Income Method

👨‍💼 Income Method

Under the Income Method, national income is calculated by adding all factor incomes earned by the factors of production during an accounting year.

Components of Factor Income

Component Meaning Examples
Compensation of Employees Payment made to employees for their services Wages, salaries and employers' social contributions
Operating Surplus Income generated from ownership of property and entrepreneurship Rent, interest and profit
Mixed Income of Self-Employed Income containing more than one type of factor income Income of small shopkeepers, farmers and self-employed persons

1. Compensation of Employees

Compensation of Employees is the total remuneration paid by employers to employees for the services provided by them.
It includes:
  • Wages and salaries in cash
  • Wages and salaries in kind
  • Employers' social contributions

2. Operating Surplus

Operating Surplus refers to factor income from property and entrepreneurship.
It generally includes:
  • Rent
  • Royalty
  • Interest
  • Profit

3. Mixed Income of Self-Employed

Mixed Income of Self-Employed is the income of self-employed persons where it is difficult to separately identify the payments for their labour, capital and entrepreneurship.
Example:

A small shopkeeper works himself, uses his own capital and manages his business. His total income contains elements of wages, interest and profit. Therefore, it is called mixed income.

Income Method Formula

NDPFC = Compensation of Employees + Operating Surplus + Mixed Income of Self-Employed
Important:

Transfer payments such as pensions, scholarships and gifts are not factor incomes because they are not payments made for current productive services.
Example:

Compensation of Employees = ₹500 crore
Operating Surplus = ₹300 crore
Mixed Income = ₹200 crore

NDPFC = ₹500 + ₹300 + ₹200

= ₹1,000 crore
10C. Expenditure Method

🛒 Expenditure Method

Under the Expenditure Method, national income is measured by adding expenditure on final goods and services made by different sectors of the economy during an accounting year.

Main Components of Expenditure

Symbol Component Meaning
C Private Final Consumption Expenditure Consumption expenditure by households
I Investment Expenditure Expenditure on capital goods and inventories
G Government Final Consumption Expenditure Government expenditure on final goods and services
X Exports Goods and services sold to other countries
M Imports Goods and services purchased from other countries

1. Private Final Consumption Expenditure (C)

Private Final Consumption Expenditure is the expenditure made by households on final goods and services for consumption purposes.
Examples:
  • Food purchased by households
  • Clothes
  • Education services
  • Medical services
  • Transport services

2. Investment Expenditure (I)

Investment expenditure refers to expenditure on capital goods and changes in inventories that add to the productive capacity or stock of the economy.
It may include:
  • Purchase of machinery
  • Construction of factories
  • Purchase of equipment
  • Change in inventories

Government Final Consumption Expenditure refers to government expenditure on final goods and services that directly satisfy collective needs.
