UNIT 1

National Income – Introduction

National Income refers to the total factor income earned by the normal residents of a country during an accounting year.

National income accounting is a systematic method of measuring the economic activities and income generated in an economy during a particular period, generally one accounting year.

It helps us understand the level of production, income and expenditure in an economy. It also provides important information about economic growth, changes in living standards, sectoral performance and the overall condition of an economy.

Core idea: Production creates income, income creates expenditure, and expenditure is spent on the goods and services produced in the economy. Therefore, production, income and expenditure are different ways of looking at the same economic activity.
BASIC CONCEPTS

Important Concepts of National Income

National Income

National Income is the sum total of factor incomes earned by the normal residents of a country during an accounting year.

Factor incomes include compensation of employees, rent, interest, profit and mixed income of self-employed persons, subject to the relevant national income accounting treatment.

Domestic Product

Domestic Product is the money value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during an accounting year.

The important point is location of production. Domestic product includes production taking place within the domestic territory irrespective of whether the producer is a resident or a non-resident.

National Product

National Product measures the value of final goods and services produced by the normal residents of a country during an accounting year.

The important point is normal residence. Therefore, income earned by residents from abroad is included, while factor income earned by non-residents from the domestic territory is excluded when moving from domestic to national.

National Product = Domestic Product + NFIA
CONCEPT

Domestic Territory

Domestic territory refers to the economic territory that is under the administrative control of a government and within which persons, goods and capital can move freely.

For national income accounting, domestic territory is not exactly the same as geographical territory. It is an economic concept.

Included examples

  • Political boundaries of the country.
  • Ships and aircraft operated by normal residents between countries.
  • Embassies and consulates of the country located abroad.

Excluded examples

  • Foreign embassies located within the country.
  • Foreign ships and aircraft operating within the country under the relevant accounting treatment.
Exam Tip: Whenever a question uses the words “within the domestic territory”, think in terms of the location of production.
CONCEPT

Normal Resident

A normal resident is a person or institution whose centre of economic interest lies in the economic territory of a country and who normally resides or operates there for a prolonged period.

Normal residence is an economic concept and is not simply determined by citizenship. A person may be a citizen of one country but may be a normal resident of another country.

Example: An Indian citizen permanently working and living abroad may not be treated as a normal resident of India for national income accounting purposes.
Remember: Citizenship ≠ Normal Residence.
CLASSIFICATION

Final Goods and Intermediate Goods

Final Goods

Final goods are goods that are purchased for final consumption or investment and are not intended for resale or further processing during the accounting period.

The value of final goods is included in the calculation of national income because including them represents the value of final output.

Intermediate Goods

Intermediate goods are goods used as inputs in the production of other goods and services during the same accounting period.
Example: Flour purchased by a bakery to make bread is an intermediate good for the bakery.
Final Goods Intermediate Goods
Used for final consumption or investment. Used for further production.
Included in final output. Normally excluded separately to avoid double counting.
Example: Bread purchased by a household. Example: Flour used by a bakery.
BASIC CONCEPT

Stock and Flow

Stock

Stock is a quantity measured at a particular point of time.

Examples include wealth, capital and inventory at a particular date.

Flow

Flow is a quantity measured over a period of time.

Examples include national income, production, consumption, saving and investment.

Easy distinction:
Stock → measured at a point of time
Flow → measured during a period of time
IMPORTANT

Depreciation

Depreciation is the loss in value of fixed capital assets due to normal wear and tear, normal obsolescence and expected damage arising from their use in production.

Machines, buildings and other fixed capital assets gradually lose their productive value. This loss is taken into account while moving from gross measures to net measures.

Net Product = Gross Product − Depreciation
Example: If GDP is ₹1,000 crore and depreciation is ₹100 crore:

NDP = ₹1,000 crore − ₹100 crore = ₹900 crore
Memory rule: Gross → subtract depreciation → Net.
CONVERSION

Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)

NFIA is the difference between factor income received from abroad and factor income paid to factors of production owned by non-residents.
NFIA = Factor Income from Abroad − Factor Income to Abroad

Components

  • Compensation of employees received from abroad minus compensation paid abroad.
  • Property and entrepreneurial income received from abroad minus similar income paid abroad.
If factor income received from abroad = ₹80 crore and factor income paid abroad = ₹50 crore:

NFIA = ₹80 crore − ₹50 crore = ₹30 crore.
Important: NFIA may be positive, zero or negative.
EIGHT AGGREGATES

National Income Aggregates

The major aggregates are classified according to three important distinctions: Domestic/National, Gross/Net and Market Price/Factor Cost.

Aggregate Meaning
GDP at MP Gross Domestic Product at Market Price
GDP at FC Gross Domestic Product at Factor Cost
NDP at MP Net Domestic Product at Market Price
NDP at FC Net Domestic Product at Factor Cost
GNP at MP Gross National Product at Market Price
GNP at FC Gross National Product at Factor Cost
NNP at MP Net National Product at Market Price
NNP at FC Net National Product at Factor Cost

Market Price and Factor Cost

Market Price is the price actually paid by the purchaser for a good or service.
Factor Cost refers to the payments made to factors of production for their contribution to the production process.

Net Indirect Taxes

Net Indirect Taxes = Indirect Taxes − Subsidies
If indirect taxes = ₹150 crore and subsidies = ₹40 crore:

NIT = ₹150 crore − ₹40 crore = ₹110 crore.
CONVERSION RULES

Conversion Between Aggregates

MP → FC : Subtract Net Indirect Taxes

FC → MP : Add Net Indirect Taxes

Gross → Net : Subtract Depreciation

Net → Gross : Add Depreciation

Domestic → National : Add NFIA

National → Domestic : Subtract NFIA

Examples

GDP at MP = ₹800 crore
NIT = ₹100 crore

GDP at FC = ₹800 − ₹100 = ₹700 crore
GDP at MP = ₹900 crore
Depreciation = ₹150 crore

NDP at MP = ₹900 − ₹150 = ₹750 crore
GDP at MP = ₹1,000 crore
NFIA = ₹80 crore

GNP at MP = ₹1,000 + ₹80 = ₹1,080 crore
IMPORTANT

Double Counting

Double counting means counting the value of the same good or service more than once while calculating national income.

It generally occurs when the value of intermediate goods is counted along with the value of final goods.

Suppose wheat is sold for ₹20, flour for ₹35 and bread for ₹60. If all three values are added:

₹20 + ₹35 + ₹60 = ₹115

This would overstate production because the value of wheat and flour is already included in the value of bread.

Methods to avoid double counting

  • Count only final goods and services.
  • Use the value added method.
Best exam line: The value of intermediate goods is not counted separately because it is already included in the value of final goods.
METHOD 1

Value Added Method

The Value Added Method measures national income by adding the value added by all producing units in the domestic economy during an accounting year.

Value added represents the additional value created by a producer during the production process. It prevents double counting because only the additional contribution of each production unit is counted.

Value Added = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption

Step-by-step process

  1. Estimate the value of output produced by each producing unit.
  2. Calculate intermediate consumption.
  3. Subtract intermediate consumption from output.
  4. Obtain gross value added.
  5. Make the required adjustments for depreciation, taxes and subsidies.
  6. Move from domestic to national concept by adding NFIA where required.

Important terms

Value of Output

It refers to the value of goods and services produced by a production unit during the accounting period. Sales and relevant changes in inventories are considered where applicable.

Intermediate Consumption

It refers to the value of goods and services used as inputs in the production process during the accounting period.

A firm produces goods worth ₹500 crore and uses intermediate goods worth ₹300 crore.

Value Added = ₹500 − ₹300
= ₹200 crore

Precautions

  • Do not count intermediate goods separately.
  • Exclude purely financial transactions.
  • Exclude transfer payments.
  • Carefully treat changes in inventories.
  • Do not count the value of second-hand goods as current production.
METHOD 2

Income Method

The Income Method measures national income by adding factor incomes earned by factors of production during the accounting year.

Factors of production receive income for participating in production. Therefore, the total factor income generated through production can be used to measure national income.

Main components

Compensation of Employees

It includes payments received by employees in return for their contribution to production. It broadly includes wages, salaries and relevant employer contributions.

Operating Surplus

Operating surplus mainly includes factor income in the form of rent, royalty, interest and profit arising from production.

Mixed Income of Self-Employed

Self-employed persons often perform several functions simultaneously. Their income may contain elements of wages, rent, interest and profit. Such income is therefore treated as mixed income.

Domestic Factor Income = Compensation of Employees + Operating Surplus + Mixed Income

Important exclusions

  • Transfer payments.
  • Windfall gains that do not arise from current production.
  • Sale of second-hand goods itself.
  • Purely financial transactions.
A production sector generates:

Compensation of Employees = ₹400 crore
Operating Surplus = ₹250 crore
Mixed Income = ₹150 crore

Domestic Factor Income = 400 + 250 + 150
= ₹800 crore
Remember: Income Method focuses on income earned by factors, not every payment received by individuals.
METHOD 3

Expenditure Method

The Expenditure Method measures national income by adding expenditure incurred on final goods and services during an accounting year.

Since production generates income and income is spent on final goods and services, total final expenditure can be used to estimate the value of final output.

Main components

Private Final Consumption Expenditure

Expenditure incurred by households and non-profit institutions on final goods and services for consumption purposes.

Government Final Consumption Expenditure

Government expenditure on final goods and services used for providing public services and satisfying collective needs.

Gross Domestic Capital Formation

It represents expenditure on capital formation within the domestic economy and includes relevant fixed capital formation and changes in inventories.

Net Exports

Net Exports = Exports − Imports

Exports represent expenditure by foreigners on domestically produced goods and services, while imports represent expenditure on goods and services produced outside the domestic economy.

GDP at MP = PFCE + GFCE + GDCF + (Exports − Imports)

Precautions

  • Count only expenditure on final goods and services.
  • Exclude expenditure on intermediate goods.
  • Exclude purely financial transactions.
  • Exclude transfer payments.
  • Correctly account for imports and exports.
  • Include relevant changes in inventories.
Suppose:

PFCE = ₹600 crore
GFCE = ₹200 crore
GDCF = ₹150 crore
Exports = ₹100 crore
Imports = ₹80 crore

GDP at MP = 600 + 200 + 150 + (100 − 80)
= ₹970 crore
COMPARISON

Three Methods at a Glance

Method Measures Through Main Focus
Value Added Method Value added by producers Production
Income Method Factor incomes Income generation
Expenditure Method Final expenditure Spending
All three methods should theoretically give the same overall result when applied correctly because production, income and expenditure are interconnected parts of the same economic activity.
DISTINCTION

Factor Income and Transfer Income

Factor Income

Factor income is income earned by factors of production for rendering productive services.

Examples include wages, rent, interest and profit.

Transfer Income

Transfer income is income received without providing any current productive service in return.

Examples may include pensions, scholarships and certain social security benefits, depending on their nature.

Factor Income Transfer Income
Arises from productive services. Does not arise from current productive services.
Included as factor income. Excluded from national income as factor income.
Example: Salary for work. Example: Certain pension payments.
DISTINCTION

Domestic Product vs National Product

Basis Domestic Product National Product
Basis Domestic territory Normal residents
Foreign factor income Relevant to domestic-national adjustment Included through NFIA
Basic question Where was production carried out? Who are the normal residents earning the factor income?
National Product = Domestic Product + NFIA
DISTINCTION

Gross and Net Concepts

The word gross means depreciation has not been deducted. The word net means depreciation has been deducted.

Net = Gross − Depreciation

Gross = Net + Depreciation
GDP = ₹1,200 crore
Depreciation = ₹200 crore

NDP = ₹1,200 − ₹200
= ₹1,000 crore
⚠️ Common Mistakes in National Income

1. Confusing Domestic and National

Domestic is based on the domestic territory, whereas national is based on the normal residents.

2. Forgetting NFIA

When converting Domestic Product into National Product, remember:

National = Domestic + NFIA

3. Wrong treatment of depreciation

Gross to Net means subtract depreciation. Net to Gross means add depreciation.

4. Wrong treatment of subsidies

NIT = Indirect Taxes − Subsidies

Subsidies are subtracted from indirect taxes while calculating net indirect taxes.

5. Counting intermediate goods

Counting intermediate goods separately can result in double counting.

6. Confusing factor income with transfer income

A payment received by a person is not automatically factor income. The payment must arise from a productive factor service.

7. Including second-hand goods as current production

The second-hand good itself was produced in an earlier period. However, current services such as brokerage or commission associated with its sale may be relevant.

8. Treating financial transactions as current production

Purchase and sale of financial assets do not themselves represent current production of goods and services.

9. Forgetting imports in expenditure method

Imports are deducted because they are included in domestic expenditure but are not produced within the domestic economy.

10. Using citizenship instead of residence

National income accounting uses the concept of normal residence rather than citizenship.

🧮 Solved Numerical Examples

Example 1 – GDP to NDP

GDP at MP = ₹2,000 crore
Depreciation = ₹250 crore

NDP at MP = GDP at MP − Depreciation

= 2,000 − 250
= ₹1,750 crore

Example 2 – GDP at MP to GDP at FC

GDP at MP = ₹1,500 crore
Indirect Taxes = ₹200 crore
Subsidies = ₹50 crore

NIT = 200 − 50 = ₹150 crore

GDP at FC = 1,500 − 150
= ₹1,350 crore

Example 3 – GDP to GNP

GDP at MP = ₹3,000 crore
Factor income from abroad = ₹180 crore
Factor income paid abroad = ₹120 crore

NFIA = 180 − 120 = ₹60 crore

GNP at MP = 3,000 + 60
= ₹3,060 crore

Example 4 – Expenditure Method

PFCE = ₹800 crore
GFCE = ₹250 crore
GDCF = ₹300 crore
Exports = ₹150 crore
Imports = ₹100 crore

GDP at MP = 800 + 250 + 300 + (150 − 100)

= 800 + 250 + 300 + 50
= ₹1,400 crore

Example 5 – Value Added

Value of output = ₹900 crore
Intermediate consumption = ₹550 crore

Value Added = 900 − 550
= ₹350 crore
NUMERICALS

How to Solve National Income Numericals

  1. Read the question carefully. Identify whether the question asks for GDP, NDP, GNP, NNP or National Income.
  2. Identify the required conversion. Check whether you need to move between domestic/national, gross/net or market price/factor cost.
  3. Write the formula first. Do not immediately start calculating.
  4. Substitute the values. Keep units such as ₹ crore or ₹ lakh consistent.
  5. Check the direction. For example, Gross → Net means depreciation is deducted.
  6. Write the final answer clearly. Mention the aggregate and unit.
Numerical checklist:
Gross/Net → Depreciation
Domestic/National → NFIA
MP/FC → NIT
Expenditure → Exports − Imports
EXAM CONCEPTS

Important Distinctions

Gross vs Net

Gross includes depreciation; net excludes depreciation.

Domestic vs National

Domestic is based on domestic territory; national is based on normal residents.

Market Price vs Factor Cost

Market price is the price paid by the purchaser, while factor cost represents factor payments for production.

Final Goods vs Intermediate Goods

Final goods are meant for final use, whereas intermediate goods are used for further production.

Stock vs Flow

Stock is measured at a point of time, while flow is measured over a period of time.

Factor Income vs Transfer Income

Factor income arises from productive services, while transfer income is received without providing current productive services in return.

Master the four adjustments: Depreciation, NFIA, Indirect Taxes and Subsidies.
UNDERSTANDING

Why National Income is Important

  • It helps measure the size and performance of an economy.
  • It helps compare economic performance over different years.
  • It provides information about production and income generation.
  • It assists governments in economic planning and policy formulation.
  • It helps analyse changes in different sectors of the economy.
  • It provides a basis for studying economic growth.
  • It helps in analysing consumption, saving and investment patterns.
  • It provides useful information for economic decision-making.
National income statistics are therefore not merely numerical calculations; they provide a structured picture of economic activity.
