📘 Fundamental Rights and DPSP

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy are two important parts of the Indian Constitution. They have different constitutional roles, but both aim to establish a just and democratic society.

Fundamental Rights mainly protect individual liberty and freedom, while DPSPs provide directions to the State for achieving social and economic justice and establishing a welfare-oriented society.

Key Idea:

Fundamental Rights and DPSPs should not be understood as completely opposite. The constitutional approach has gradually developed towards maintaining a balance and harmony between them.

⚖️ Fundamental Rights vs DPSP

Fundamental Rights Directive Principles
Contained in Part III Contained in Part IV
Articles 12–35 Articles 36–51
Generally enforceable by courts Not enforceable by courts
Protect individual rights and freedoms Guide the State towards social and economic welfare
Act as important limitations on State action Provide constitutional directions to the State
Important for political and individual liberty Important for social and economic democracy

📜 Article 37 — Why DPSPs Are Different

Article 37
Constitutional Provision

“The provisions contained in this Part shall not be enforceable by any court, but the principles therein laid down are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country and it shall be the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.”

Explanation

Article 37 makes it clear that a person generally cannot approach a court simply to demand implementation of a Directive Principle. Therefore, DPSPs are called non-justiciable.

However, this does not mean that DPSPs are unimportant. The Constitution itself describes them as fundamental in the governance of the country and places a duty on the State to apply them while making laws.

⚔️ How Did the Conflict Arise?

A constitutional question gradually arose when the State attempted to implement social and economic objectives reflected in DPSPs, but the method used for doing so was challenged as violating a Fundamental Right.

The basic question was:

If a law or government policy is intended to achieve a DPSP but violates a Fundamental Right, what should happen?

The Supreme Court's approach developed over time. The following landmark cases show this constitutional journey.

⚖️ Case 1 — State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan

Year: 1951

Starting Point
📌 Actual Issue

The Madras Government had issued a policy known as the Communal G.O. for admissions to government medical and engineering colleges.

Seats were distributed among different communities according to a fixed communal pattern. Srimathi Champakam Dorairajan challenged the policy, arguing that it violated her Fundamental Rights.

⚖️ Constitutional Provisions Involved

Article 29(2) — No citizen can be denied admission to a State-maintained or State-aided educational institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste, language or any of them.

Article 46 — Directs the State to promote, with special care, the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly SCs and STs.

🔗 Where Was the Conflict?

The State's objective was connected with advancing the interests of educationally backward sections, an objective reflected in Article 46.

However, the method used to achieve that objective was challenged under Article 29(2).

So the constitutional tension was:

Article 46 (DPSP) → promotion of weaker sections

vs.

Article 29(2) (Fundamental Right) → protection against denial of admission on specified grounds.

🏛️ Supreme Court's Decision

The Supreme Court held that the Communal G.O. violated Article 29(2) and was therefore unconstitutional.

The Court also held that the Directive Principles could not override the Fundamental Rights contained in Part III.

🎯 Final Result

The Communal G.O. was struck down.

At this stage of constitutional development, where a direct conflict existed, Fundamental Rights prevailed over DPSPs.

📚 Why This Case Matters

This case became an important starting point in the judicial discussion about the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.

It also contributed to the constitutional developments that led to the insertion of Article 15(4) through the First Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951.

⚖️ Case 2 — In re Kerala Education Bill

Year: 1958

📌 Actual Issue

The Kerala Education Bill, 1957 contained provisions concerning the regulation and administration of educational institutions.

The Bill was connected with the constitutional objective of providing education, particularly the objective reflected in Article 45.

At the same time, some provisions were challenged as interfering with the Fundamental Rights of minority educational institutions under Article 30(1).

⚖️ Constitutional Provisions Involved

Article 45 — Directive relating to free and compulsory education for children in the constitutional framework then applicable.

Article 30(1) — Gives religious and linguistic minorities the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

🔗 Where Was the Conflict?

The State argued that the educational measures were intended to advance constitutional objectives.

Minority institutions, however, argued that certain provisions would interfere with their Fundamental Right to establish and administer educational institutions.

Therefore, the Court had to consider how the constitutional objective of education could operate alongside the Fundamental Rights of minorities.

🏛️ Supreme Court's Decision

The Court examined the provisions individually and found that certain provisions did interfere with the rights guaranteed by Article 30(1), while other provisions did not.

The Court also moved towards the principle of harmonious construction — an attempt should be made to give effect to both Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles rather than automatically treating them as completely opposed.

🎯 Final Result

The case helped develop the idea that Fundamental Rights and DPSPs should, as far as possible, be interpreted in a way that allows both to operate.

📚 Why This Case Matters

It represents an important movement from the earlier approach in Champakam Dorairajan towards the idea of harmony between Part III and Part IV.

⚖️ Case 3 — Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala

Year: 1973

📌 Actual Issue

The case arose against the background of Kerala land reform legislation and constitutional amendments that sought to protect certain laws from challenges based on Fundamental Rights.

The case therefore involved a major constitutional question: How far can Parliament go in changing the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights?

⚖️ Constitutional Provisions & DPSP Connection

The case involved the constitutional protection given to laws implementing certain Directive Principles, particularly Article 39(b) and Article 39(c), through Article 31C.

It also involved Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368.

🔗 Where Was the Conflict?

The larger constitutional tension was between laws intended to advance social and economic objectives and the Fundamental Rights against which such laws could be challenged.

The Court therefore had to consider not only the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs but also whether Parliament could use its amending power to alter the constitutional balance.

🏛️ Supreme Court's Decision

By a 7–6 majority, the Supreme Court held that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is wide, but it cannot destroy or alter its Basic Structure.

The Court also considered the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs and recognised the importance of attempting a harmonious interpretation of the two.

🎯 Final Result

The Basic Structure Doctrine was established.

The constitutional balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles became an important consideration in constitutional interpretation.

📚 Why This Case Matters

Kesavananda Bharati is important because it placed a constitutional limit on Parliament's amending power and became a major foundation for later decisions concerning the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.

📜 Article 31C — Important Constitutional Connection

Constitutional Provision

Article 31C was introduced by the 25th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971. It originally provided protection to laws giving effect to the policy towards securing the principles specified in Article 39(b) and Article 39(c) from challenges under Articles 14 and 19, subject to its constitutional conditions.

Explanation

Article 31C was created because Parliament wanted to give stronger constitutional protection to certain laws intended to achieve important social and economic objectives under the DPSPs.

The provision therefore became one of the most important constitutional links between Part III and Part IV.

📌 Important Development

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 attempted to expand Article 31C protection from Article 39(b) and 39(c) to all Directive Principles.

This expansion was later struck down by the Supreme Court in Minerva Mills.

⚖️ Case 4 — Minerva Mills v. Union of India

Year: 1980

📌 Actual Issue

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 attempted to expand Article 31C so that laws made to implement any DPSP would receive protection from challenges under certain Fundamental Rights.

This raised a major constitutional question: Can DPSPs be given such complete priority that Fundamental Rights lose their constitutional protection?

⚖️ Constitutional Provisions Involved

The dispute involved the relationship between Part III (Fundamental Rights) and Part IV (DPSPs).

The expanded version of Article 31C was especially important.

🔗 Where Was the Conflict?

The constitutional amendment attempted to give laws implementing all DPSPs a very broad protection against certain Fundamental Rights challenges.

In practical terms, this would have greatly increased the priority given to Part IV over Part III.

The Court therefore had to consider whether the Constitution permitted one part to be given such overwhelming superiority over the other.

🏛️ Supreme Court's Decision

The Supreme Court struck down the expansion of Article 31C made by the 42nd Amendment.

The Court emphasised that Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles are both important parts of the constitutional scheme and should be understood through balance and harmony.

🎯 Final Result

The attempt to give blanket priority to all DPSPs over Fundamental Rights was invalidated.

The Court held that the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is an important feature of the Constitution's Basic Structure.

📚 Why This Case Matters

Minerva Mills provides the clearest expression of the modern constitutional approach:

Fundamental Rights + DPSPs = Constitutional Harmony

The Constitution should not be understood as requiring the complete victory of Part III over Part IV or Part IV over Part III.

🔗 Present Constitutional Position

The judicial approach has developed from an early position in which Fundamental Rights were treated as prevailing in direct conflict with DPSPs towards an approach that seeks to give effect to both Parts together.

DPSPs cannot simply be used as a general excuse to violate Fundamental Rights. At the same time, Fundamental Rights should be interpreted in a manner that does not unnecessarily prevent the State from pursuing legitimate constitutional goals reflected in the Directive Principles.

⭐ Remember:

The modern approach is not simply “Fundamental Rights vs DPSP.”

It is:
Fundamental Rights + DPSPs → Harmony, Balance and Constitutional Governance.

📅 Judicial Evolution — Quick Timeline

1951 — Champakam Dorairajan

Fundamental Rights prevailed where there was a direct conflict with DPSPs.

1958 — Kerala Education Bill

The Court moved towards harmonious construction of Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.

1973 — Kesavananda Bharati

Basic Structure Doctrine established; constitutional balance became a major principle.

1980 — Minerva Mills

The Court rejected giving blanket priority to DPSPs and emphasised harmony between Part III and Part IV.

🎯 Final Takeaway

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles represent two sides of the constitutional vision.

Fundamental Rights protect individual liberty, while DPSPs guide the State towards social and economic justice.

One-line conclusion:

Neither Fundamental Rights nor DPSPs should be treated as enemies; the constitutional objective is to maintain a workable balance and harmony between both.
